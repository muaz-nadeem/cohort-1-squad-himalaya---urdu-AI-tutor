Metabolic activities within body cells generate a range of waste products, many of which can become
harmful if allowed to accumulate. To prevent toxicity, these wastes must be efficiently removed. Body fluids such
as blood and lymph serve as transport mediums, carrying waste materials away from active tissues to excretory
organs.

Different systems play distinct roles in excretion. The respiratory system eliminates carbon dioxide, a major
waste product of cellular respiration, from the bloodstream. The urinary system is responsible for filtering blood
to remove nitrogenous wastes (such as urea), excess salts, and water.

In addition to excretion, the urinary system performs several vital regulatory functions. It maintains the
osmotic balance of body fluids by adjusting water and electrolyte concentrations, regulates blood pH and fluid
volume, and contributes to the control of blood pressure and red blood cell production through the secretion of
hormones like renin and erythropoietin.

NITROGENOUS COMPOUNDS EXCRETED DURING EXCRETION.

The breakdown of proteins and nucleic acids in the body produces nitrogenous wastes. These substances
are toxic if allowed to accumulate, so they must be removed efficiently through the process of excretion. The main
nitrogenous compounds excreted by humans include urea, uric acid, creatinine, and small amounts of ammonia.
Each of these compounds originates from different metabolic processes and is eliminated mainly through urine.

1. Urea

Urea is the primary nitrogenous waste in humans. It is formed in the liver through the urea cycle, where
ammonia (a highly toxic by-product of protein breakdown) is converted into a less toxic, water-soluble form.
Urea is then transported by the blood to the kidneys and excreted in urine. On average, about 80-90% of
the nitrogen excreted from the body is in the form of urea. For example, after eating a protein-rich meal,
urea levels in the blood and urine rise because more amino acids are broken down.

2. Uric Acid

Uric acid is produced during the breakdown of nucleic acids (DNA and RNA). It is less soluble in water
compared to urea. Normally, only a small quantity of uric acid is excreted in urine, but if its concentration
becomes too high, it can crystallize and deposit in joints, causing a painful disorder called gout. Birds and
reptiles excrete uric acid as their main nitrogenous waste, which conserves water since it is eliminated as a
paste-like substance.

3. Creatinine

Creatinine is formed in musc'es as a by-product of the breakdown of creatine phosphate, a compound that
stores energy for muscle contractions. It is produced at a relatively constant rate, depending on muscle
activity and mass. Because the kidneys efficiently filter creatinine from the blood, measuring its level in urine
or blood is an important clinical test of kidney function. Elevated creatinine levels may indicate impaired
kidney performance.

4. Ammonia

Ammonia is a direct by-product of amino acid metabolism. It is extremely toxic and must be removed quickly
from the body. In humans, most ammonia is converted into urea in the liver before excretion. However,
small amounts of free ammonia are excreted directly into urine, where they also help maintain the acid—
base balance of the body.

5. Other Nitrogenous Compounds

In addition to the main wastes, trace amounts of other nitrogen-containing substances such as amino acids,

peptides, and nitrogenous bases may also appear in urine. Their presence in significant amounts, however,

can be a sign of metabolic or renal disorders. For instance, excessive amino acids in urine (aminoaciduria)
may indicate defects in amino acid metabolism.

Doctors often perform a urinalysis to measure nitrogenous waste levels. For example, the blood urea
nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine test are routinely used to evaluate kidney health. Elevated levels of urea or
creatinine in blood may signal kidney disease, dehydration, or other metabolic problems. The nitrogenous
compounds excreted during excretion include urea (major form), uric acid, creatinine, and small amounts of
ammonia. Together, these wastes help the body maintain chemical balance and prevent toxic buildup. “

EXCRETORY PRODUCTS AND THEIR RELATION TO HABITATS

Different animals excrete nitrogenous wastes in forms that reflect both their metabolism and the availability
of water in their environment. The three main types of excretory products are ammonia, urea, and uric acid, and
each type is associated with particular habitats and survival strategies. This adaptation ensures that organisms
can efficiently remove toxic wastes while conserving water and energy.

1. Ammonotelic Animals: Ammonia Excretion

Ammonia is the most primitive and highly toxic nitrogenous waste. Because it is very soluble in water, it

must be excreted quickly and ina diluted form. Therefore, animals that live in aquatic habitats, where water

is abundantly available, usually excrete ammonia. To safely excrete just one gram of nitrogen as ammonia,
an organism requires approximately 500 mL of water, making this strategy feasible only for organisms in
water-rich environments.

¢ Examples: Many freshwater invertebrates and vertebrates, such as protozoans, sponges, cnidarians,

echinoderms, crustaceans, and most bony fishes, amphibians, and aquatic animals, adopt this method
of excretion. These organisms are termed ammonotelic.

Urea is less toxic than ammonia and can be stored in the body for a short time before being excreted. It is

¢ Examples: Humans and most mammals (e.g., dogs, lions, cows) are ureotelic.

¢ Advantage: Reduces water loss while maintaining effective waste removal.

© Real-life link: Desert mammals like the kangaroo rat concentrate their urine to conserve maximum
water while excreting urea.

Uricotelic Animals: Uric Acid Excretion

Uric acid is the least toxic form of nitrogenous waste and is excreted as a paste or crystals, requiring very

little water. This adaptation is highly beneficial in arid environments, where water is scarce, and for animals

that develop within shelled eggs, where nitrogenous wastes cannot be excreted in liquid form.

¢ Examples: Birds, reptiles, and insects are uricotelic.

e Advantage: Saves water and prevents accumulation of soluble toxic waste inside the egg.

e Real-life link: The white paste often seen in bird droppings is uric acid.

Where do you think the carbon dioxide used in the formation of urea comes from? Where
does the remainder of excess carbon dioxide go to be excreted?

The carbon dioxide involved in urea formation comes from cellular respiration. Inside the
mitochondria of body cells carbohydrates, fats, and proteins are broken down to produce
energy (ATP). A majo; :-y-product of this process is carbon dioxide (C02).

In the urea cycle (whic. mainly takes place in the liver), some of this CO2 combines with
ammonia (NHs) to form carbamoyl phosphate, which then enters the cycle and eventually
contributes to the production of urea: Urea is later excreted in urine.

The remainder of the excess CO2, which is not used in the urea cycle, does not stay in the liver.
Instead, it dissolves in the blood, is carried to the lungs (mainly as bicarbonate fons, HCOs*),

and is exhaled during breathing.

STRUCTURE OF THE KIDNEY

The kidneys are the primary excretory organs of humans, responsible for filtering blood, maintaining water

and electrolyte balance, and excreting nitrogenous wastes. Each kidney is a highly specialized organ with a
complex structure that supports these vital functions. Understanding the structure of the kidney is crucial for
appreciating how it performs its role in excretion and homeostasis.

1.

External Structure
Shape, Size, and Location:

Each kidney is bean-shaped, reddish-brown in color, and measures about 10-12 cm in length, 5-7 cm in
width, and 3-4 cm in thickness in adults. They weigh approximately 120-150 grams each. The kidneys are
located in the posterior abdominal cavity on either side of the vertebral column, just below the ribcage. The
right kidney is usually slightly lower than the left due to the presence of the liver.

STRUCTURE OF A NEPHRON

The nephron is the fundamental structural and functional unit of the kidney, responsible for the filtration of
blood and the formation of urine. Each human kidney contains approximately 1 to 1.5 million nephrons, which
operate collectively to maintain fluid and electrolyte balance, remove waste products, and regulate blood pH.
Structure of the Nephron

Atypical nephron consists of two main parts:

e Renal corpuscle

¢ Renal tubule
1. Renal Corpuscle

The renal corpuscle is located in the renal cortex and is the site of initial blood filtration. It comprises:

* Glomerulus — A dense, tuft-like network of capillaries that receives blood from the afferent arteriole.

* Bowman’s capsule — A double-walled, cup-like structure that encases the glomerulus. It marks the

beginning of the renal tubule and collects the filtrate from the glomerular capillaries.
2. Renal Tubule and Associated Structures

After the filtrate is collected in Bowman's capsule, it passes through a series of specialized tubules where

selective reabsorption and secretion occur:

a. Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT)

Immediately following Bowman’s capsule, the filtrate enters the proximal convoluted tubule, a highly
coiled segment located in the cortex. This region is responsible for the reabsorption of water, ions,
glucose, and amino acids into the surrounding capillaries.

b. Loop of Henle

The PCT descends into the renal medulla to form the loop of Henle, which has two distinct limbs:

© Descending limb — Permeable to water, facilitating water reabsorption.

© Ascending limb — impermeable to water but actively transports salts out of the filtrate.

This arrangement enables the kidney to concentrate urine through the countercurrent multiplier mechanism.
¢. Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT)

As the loop of Henle ascends back toward the cortex, it transitions into the distal convoluted tubule,

another coiled segment. The DCT fine-tunes ion concentration and contributes to acid-base balance and

secretion of certain wastes.

KIDNEYS AS ORGANS OF EXCRETION AND OSMOREGULATION

The kidneys are vital organs of the urinary system that play a dual role in maintaining the body’s internal
balance. They function not only as excretory organs, removing waste products of metabolism, but also as
osmoregulatory organs, regulating the concentration of water and salts in body fluids. These two roles are closely
linked, as efficient excretion requires controlled regulation of fluid and electrolyte balance.

Kidneys as Excretory Organs :

Excretion is the process of removing metabolic wastes from the body. The kidneys filter blood through
specialized structures called nephrons, which separate useful substances from harmful wastes.
1. Removal of Nitrogenous Wastes:

¢ Proteins and nucleic acids are metabolized to produce nitrogenous wastes such as urea, creatinine, and

uric acid.

* These compounds are toxic if allowed to accumulate, so the kidneys filter them out and excrete them in

urine.
2. Detoxification of the Blood:

© Drugs, toxins, and by-products of metabolism (e.g., ammonia converted to urea in the liver) are

eliminated.

¢ This purification ensures that blood leaving the kidneys is free from harmful metabolic end products.
3. Acid-Base Balance:

¢ By excreting hydrogen ions (H*) and reabsorbing bicarbonate (HCOs*), kidneys maintain the blood pH

within a narrow range (around 7.35-7.45).

© Without this, metabolic acidosis or alkalosis could impair cellular functions.

Thus, the kidneys serve as highly efficient filters, protecting the body from toxic buildup and maintaining
chemical stability.

Kidneys as Osmoregulatory Organs
Osmoregulation refers to the control of water and salt balance in the body to ensure homeostasis. Kidneys
maintain the osmotic pressure of blood plasma and regulate extracellular fluid composition.
1. Regulation of Water Balance:
e Theantidiuretic hormone (ADH) increases the permeability of the collecting ducts, allowing more water
to be reabsorbed into the bloodstream during dehydration.
e  Inconditions of excess water intake, less ADH is secreted, resulting in dilute urine.
2. Regulation of Salt (Electrolyte) Balance:
e The kidneys control the levels of sodium, potassium, chloride, and calcium ions.

® The hormone aldosterone,. secreted by the adrenal cortex, promotes sodium reabsorption and
potassium excretion in the distal tubule, which also indirectly regulates water retention.

3. Maintenance of Blood Pressure and Volume:
© Specialized kidney cells release renin, which triggers the renin-angiotensin—aldosterone system (RAAS).
This pathway increases blood pressure by vasoconstriction and stimulating sodium and water
reabsorption.
e Inthis way, kidneys play a central role in long-term regulation of blood pressure.
> Integration of Excretion and Osmoregulation
The excretory and osmoregulatory functions of kidneys are interdependent. For example:
When waste products are excreted, water is also removed. The kidneys must regulate the amount of
water lost to prevent dehydration.
© Similarly, regulation of sodium ions not only maintains osmotic balance but also determines the volume
of urine produced and the concentration of wastes.
This dual role allows the kidneys to maintain homeostasis — a stable internal environment despite external
changes. For instance, during intense exercise, increased metabolic wastes (urea, lactic acid) must be
excreted, while at the same time water and electrolytes lost in sweat must be balanced by kidney regulation.

URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS (UTIS) AND THEIR CAUSATIVE BACTERIA

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most frequent bacterial infections in humans, affecting millions
of people worldwide each year. The urinary system, which includes the kidneys, ureters, bladder, and urethra, is
normally kept sterile by various defense mechanisms such as regular urination, mucosal barriers, and immune
responses. However, when bacteria gain entry and begin to multiply within this system, infection occurs. UTIs are
more common in women due to their shorter urethra and its close proximity to the anal opening, which facilitates
the transfer of bacteria.

From a medical perspective, UTIs can be classified according to the site of infection: urethritis (infection of
the urethra), cystitis (infection of the bladder), and pyelonephritis (infection of the kidneys). In some cases,
bacteria may be present in urine without obvious symptoms; this condition is called asymptomatic bacteriuria.
Each type of infection has distinct clinical significance and is caused by characteristic groups of bacteria.

1. Urethritis

Urethritis refers to the infection of the urethra, the narrow tube that carries urine from the bladder to the

DIALYSIS: MECHANISM

Renal dialysis is a medical procedure used to remove toxins, excess water, and waste products from the
blood when the kidneys can no longer perform these vital functions adequately. Although dialysis mimics the
natural function of kidneys, it is comparatively less efficient and cannot entirely replicate their complex processes.
There are two primary types of dialysis: haemodialysis and peritoneal dialysis.

Haemodialysis

Haemodialysis involves the removal of waste materials and excess fluids by circulating the patient’s blood
through an external filtration system known as a dialyzer. This system uses tubes made of semipermeable
membranes that allow the selective exchange of substances.

S$ studyplusplus.com S$


--- Page 27 ---

A catheter is inserted into a large blood vessel, usually in the arm, through which blood is withdrawn and
passed into the dialysis machine. The blood pump directs the blood into the dialyzer from the top. Inside the
dialyzer, blood flows through synthetic cellophane tubing, while a special fluid called dialysate flows around these
tubes in the opposite direction. The dialysate, which does not contain waste products, draws out urea, creatinine,
excess ions, and other metabolic wastes from the blood via diffusion across the semipermeable membrane.

Once filtered, the cleaned blood exits from the bottom of the machine and is returned to the patient's body
through a second catheter. The used dialysate exits from the top of the machine, carrying the filtered waste
materials with it.

Peritoneal Dialysis

Peritoneal dialysis utilizes the body's own peritoneal membrane—a natural semipermeable lining of the
abdominal cavity—as the filtration surface. In this method, two surgically placed catheters serve as channels for
the introduction and removal of dialysate.

During the process, dialysate is infused into the peritoneal cavity. As blood flows through the capillaries
lining the peritoneum, waste products and excess ions move across the membrane into the dialysate by diffusion
and osmosis. After a set dwell time, the fluid containing these wastes is drained out and replaced with fresh
dialysate. This exchange may be repeated several times a day, depending on the patient's clinical needs.

While dialysis is life-saving, it is not a perfect substitute for kidney function and is associated with several

1. Medical Complications

e Hypotension: Rapid removal of fluid during hemodialysis can cause sudden drops in blood pressure.

e Electrolyte Imbalances: Incorrect dialysate composition may lead to dangerous levels of potassium,

¢ Anemia and Bone Disease: Dialysis cannot replace the kidney’s hormonal functions, such as producing

e Dietary Restrictions: Patients must limit intake of fluids, salt, potassium, and phosphorus.

KIDNEY TRANSPLANT: PRINCIPLES AND ASSOCIATED PROBLEMS

When kidneys fail permanently and can no longer perform their vital functions of excretion and
osmoregulation, patients require artificial support through dialysis or surgical replacement of the kidney. Kidney
transplantation is the most effective long-term treatment for end-stage renal disease (ESRD). It involves surgically
placing a healthy kidney from a donor into a patient whose kidneys are no longer functioning. Unlike dialysis,
which only partially replaces kidney function, transplantation restores most natural kidney activities, including
hormone production, waste removal, and osmoregulation.

Principles of Kidney Transplant

Kidney transalantation is the most effective long-term treatment for patients suffering from end-stage renal
failure. However, the success of this procedure depends on several biological, medical, and surgical principles.
Understanding these principles helps explain why some transplants succeed for decades, while others fail within

a short time.

1. Donor-Recipient Matching
The most crucial factor in a successful kidney transplant is ensuring compatibility between the donor and
recipient.

Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) Matching: The HLA system is a group of proteins found’on the surface
of white blood cells and other tissues. They act like "biological ID cards" that help the immune system
distinguish between self and non-self. A closer HLA match between donor and recipient reduces the risk
of the body rejecting the transplanted kidney. For example, siblings often make better donors because
they share more HLA genes.

Blood Group Compatibility: Matching the ABO blood group system (A, B, AB, O) between donor and
recipient is equally important. A mismatch in blood type can trigger immediate rejection (hyperacute
rejection), making transplantation impossible.

Careful donor-recipient matching is therefore the foundation of transplant success.
Source of Donor Kidneys
Kidneys for transplantation can come from either living donors or deceased donors.

Living Donors: Often, close relatives such as parents, siblings, or even children donate a kidney. Living
donor kidneys are usually healthier and function more effectively after transplantation. They also allow
the surgery to be planned in advance, minimizing waiting time. Importantly, humans can live normally
with one kidney, making living donation safe in most cases.

Deceased Donors: These are individuals who have suffered brain death or cardiac death but whose
kidneys are still healthy. Such kidneys.can be transplanted if the individual consented to organ donation
during their lifetime, or if their family permits it after death. Deceased donor programs expand the pool
of available kidneys but outcomes may sometimes be slightly less favorable than with living donors.

In countries like Pakistan, deceased donor programs are limited, which adds to the shortage of kidneys and
prolongs patient waiting times.

Surgical Procedure

The surgical placement of a transplanted kidney follows specific principles to ensure both function and safety.

Placement of the Kidney: The new kidney is placed in the lower abdomen (iliac fossa), near the pelvis,
rather than in the original position of the natural kidneys. This location provides easier access to blood
vessels and the bladder.

Connection of Blood Vessels: The donor kidney’s renal artery and renal vein are surgically attached to
the recipient's iliac artery and iliac vein, ensuring proper blood flow.

Connection of the Ureter: The donor kidney’s ureter is connected directly to the recipient's bladder,
allowing urine to pass out normally.

Retention of Diseased Kidneys: In most cases, the patient's damaged kidneys are not removed. They
are left in place unless they cause complications such as recurrent infections, severe hypertension, or
kidney cancers.

This surgical strategy minimizes risks and ensures the transplanted kidney can function quickly after blood
flow is restored.

Immunosuppression

Since the transplanted kidney comes from another individual, the recipient’s immune system naturally
recognizes it as foreign. Without intervention, the immune system would attack the graft, leading to
rejection. To prevent this, patients must take immunosuppressive drugs for life.

Common Immunosuppressants: Drugs such as cyclosporine, tacrolimus, azathioprine, and
corticosteroids are given in combination. These reduce the activity of immune cells that would
otherwise damage the graft.

Balancing Risks: While these drugs are essential for graft survival, they also weaken the patient's
defense against infections and cancers. Doctors must carefully adjust doses to balance the risk of
rejection with the side effects of long-term immunosuppression.

Without immunosuppressive therapy, no kidney transplant could succeed.

Problems Associated with Kidney Transplant

Kidney transplantation is considered the gold standard treatment for end-stage renal failure because it

restores natural kidney function more effectively than dialysis. However, despite its advantages, it is not free from
challenges. Medical, surgical, and social complications can affect the success of the procedure and the patient's

. long-term survival. The following are the major problems associated with kidney transplantation:
1.

Organ Rejection

The immune system is designed to protect the body from foreign invaders..Unfortunately, a transplanted

kidney is also recognized as “foreign,” even if it comes from a close relative. This immune attack is known as

rejection.

e  Hyperacute Rejection: This occurs within minutes to hours after transplantation if the donor and
recipient tissues are poorly matched. The patient's antibodies attack the kidney immediately, causing
clotting within the blood vessels of the graft. Hyperacute rejection is rare today because careful tissue
and blood matching is done before surgery.

e Acute Rejection: This typically develops days to weeks after the operation. It is caused by the recipient's
immune cells (T lymphocytes) attacking the kidney tissue. Modern immunosuppressive drugs can often
reverse acute rejection if detected early.

@ Chronic Rejection: This is a slow, progressive rejection that occurs months or years after transplantation.
The blood vessels of the transplanted kidney gradually thicken and scar tissue forms, leading to a steady
decline in kidney function. Unlike acute rejection, chronic rejection is usually irreversible and is the
leading cause of long-term graft failure.

Even with strong immunosuppressive therapy, rejection remains the most serious complication of kidney

transplantation and requires lifelong monitoring.

Side Effects of Immunosuppressive Drugs

To prevent rejection, patients must take immunosuppressive drugs for life. While. these medicines: are

lifesaving, they also create new health challenges:

® Weak Immune Defense: Immunosuppressants reduce the body’s ability to fight infections. Patients are
more prone to bacterial, viral, and fungal infections, including pneumonia, urinary tract infections, and

shingles. Even minor infections can become life-threatening.

© Delayed Healing: Surgical wounds and minor injuries take longer to heal because of weakened immune
activity.

e Cancer Risk: Prolonged suppression of immunity increases the likelihood of certain cancers, especially
skin cancers and lymphomas.

Metabolic Side Effects: Drugs such as corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors may cause high blood
pressure, diabetes, osteoporosis (bone weakness), and excessive hair growth.

¢ Thus, while immunosuppressive drugs protect the graft, they also create a delicate balance between
preventing rejection and maintaining overall health.

Shortage of Donor Kidneys

The demand for kidney transplantation far exceeds the supply of donor organs worldwide.

¢ Long Waiting Lists: Thousands of patients remain dependent on dialysis for years, waiting for a
compatible kidney. In many countries, the waiting period can stretch from 3 to 7 years.

* Limited Donor Sources: Living donors (usually relatives) are more successful, but not always available.
Deceased donor programs exist, but cultural beliefs and lack of awareness about orga. donation often
limit participation.

e Ethical and Social Concerns: The shortage has led to unethical practices such as illegal organ trade and
exploitation of the poor, especially in developing countries. International guidelines now Promote strict
regulation of organ donation to prevent exploitation.

This shortage remains a major barrier to successful transplantation programs, particularly in countries like

Pakistan where awareness about cadaveric donation is still low.

Surgical and Medical Complications

As with any major surgery, kidney transplantation carries inherent risks, both during and after the procedure.

«Intraoperative Risks: Complications during surgery may include bleeding, blood clots, or injury to
surrounding structures. These are managed with careful surgical techniques and monitoring.

e Postoperative Infections: Surgical wounds and urinary tract infections are common due to
immunosuppression.

--- Page 31 ---

® Delayed Graft Function (DGF): In some cases, the transplanted kidney does not start working
immediately. The patient may need dialysis in the first few days or weeks until the new kidney recovers.

e Recurrence of Original Kidney Disease: In certain conditions (e.g., glomerulonephritis), the disease that
damaged the original kidneys can also affect the transplanted one.

Kidney transplantation has dramatically improved survival and quality of life for patients with ESRD. In

Pakistan and many other countries, however, limited availability of donors, high costs, and lack of awareness

about organ donation programs restrict access to this treatment. Patients often rely on living related donors,

which raises ethical and medical challenges.

Modern advances such as paired kidney exchange programs (where families swap donors for better

compatibility) and xenotransplantation research (using genetically modified animal organs) may help

address the shortage in the future.
