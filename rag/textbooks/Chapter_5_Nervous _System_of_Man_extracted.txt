The nervous system of man is a highly specialized and complex network that controls and coordinates all
activities of the body. It enables the body to sense changes in the internal and external environment and respond
to them appropriately, thereby maintaining balance and ensuring survival. The nervous system carries out three
essential roles: it detects stimuli through sense organs, processes and interprets the information in the brain and
spinal cord, and initiates suitable responses by stimulating muscles or glands. Structurally, it is divided into the
central nervous system, which includes the brain and spinal cord and is responsible for integration and control,
and the peripheral nervous system, which consists of nerves and ganglia and connects the central nervous system
with the rest of the body. In addition, the autonomic nervous system regulates involuntary activities such as
heartbeat, breathing, and digestion, thus ensuring the smooth functioning of vital organs. Altogether, the nervous
system serves as the body’s main control and communication center, making it possible for humans to think, learn,

their environm:

RECEPTORS AS BIOLOGICAL TRANSDUCERS

What is a Transducer?

A transducer is any device that can convert energy from one form to another. In engineering, microphones
convert sound into electrical signals and solar panels convert light into electricity. Similarly, in biology, receptors
act as natural transducers that convertenvironmental energies — such as light, sound, temperature, pressure, or
chemicals — into electrochemical impulses that the nervous system can understand.
> Definition of Keceptor

A receptor is a cell, tissue, or organ capable of detecting a stimulus in the environment and converting it
into an electrical message (nerve impulse). These impulses are then carried by sensory neurons to the central
nervous system (CNS), where they are interpreted and transformed into appropriate responses.

> Types of Stimuli

Receptors can detect both internal stimuli (arising inside the body) and external stimuli (coming from the

surroundings).

© Internal Stimuli: Any change occurring inside the organism is called an internal stimulus. Examples
include hunger, thirst, fluctuations in blood pH, blood pressure, or the presence of pathogens such as
during a viral infection. Detecting such stimuli helps the body maintain homeostasis.

* External Stimuli: Any change in the external environment is called an external stimulus. Examples
include light, sound, heat, touch, and mechanical pressure. Detecting such stimuli allows the organism
to interact effectively with its environment and ensures survival,

> Structural Organization of Receptors

Receptors exist in two main forms:

1. Specialized Receptors in Sense Organs: These are highly organized and sensitive to specific stimuli. For
example, photoreceptors in the retina detect light, mechanoreceptors in the inner ear detect sound and
balance, and chemoreceptors in the nose and tongue detect smell and taste.

2. Receptors Dispersed in Body Tissues: These are scattered throughout the body, such as
mechanoreceptors and thermoreceptors in the skin (for touch, pain, and temperature) or baroreceptors
in blood vessels (for blood pressure regulation). They provide continuous feedback to maintain internal
balance.

> Examples of Major Receptor Types

* Photoreceptors: Detect light (e.g., rods and cones in the retina).

¢ Mechanoreceptors: Detect pressure, vibration, touch, and sound (e.g., skin receptors, ear hair cells).

* Thermoreceptors: Detect changes in temperature (e.g., in skin and hypothalamus).

« Chemoreceptors: Detect chemicals (e.g,, taste buds, olfactory cells, oxygen receptors in blood vessels).

* Nociceptors: Detect pain caused by harmful stimull (e.g., tissue damage).

PATH OF A MESSAGE TO THE CENTRAL NERVOUS SYSTEM

The central nervous system (CNS) cannot respond to the environment unless it receives information from

receptors. The journey of this information — called sensory transmission — begins at the receptor, travels along
sensory pathways, and ends in the brain, where it is interpreted. This organized flow ensures that the body can
detect, analyze, and respond effectively to both internal and external changes.

Transmission of Sensory Information: Neural Pathway to the Brain

Sensory perception begins with the detection of stimuli and progresses through a highly organized neural

pathway to reach the brain, where the information is interpreted. This pathway usually follows a three-neuron
relay system:

1.

First-Order Neuron (Sensory Neuron)

The first-order neuron is the initial component of the sensory pathway. It originates in peripheral tissues
such as the skin, muscles, joints, or internal organs and transmitsimpiilses to the CNS. These neurons detect
various stimuli including touch, pain, pressure, temperature, and body position (proprioception) through
specialized receptor cells.

The cell bodies of first-order neurons are found in dorsal root ganglia (for the body) or cranial nerve ganglia
(for the head region). Their axons synapse with second-order neurons in the spinal cord or brainstem.
Example: When you touch a sharp pin, receptors in the skin activate first-order sensory neurons, which carry
the impulse toward the spinal cord.

Second-Order Neuron (Interneuron)

The second-order neuron receives input from the first-order neuron. It is usually located in the dorsal horn
of the spinal cord or in the brainstem, depending on the pathway. Its main function is to relay and
sometimes integrate sensory input before forwarding it to higher brain centers.

In most pathways, the axons of second-order neurons decussate (cross over) to the opposite side of the CNS
and then ascend to the thalamus, which acts as the main relay station for sensory information. ~
Example: In pain perception, the second-order neuron in the spinal cord transmits the impulse to the
thalamus after crossing to the opposite side. This explains why the left side of the brain processes pain from
the right side of the body.

The third-order neuron resides in the thalamus and serves as the final relay in the ascending sensory
pathway. It projects axons to the primary somatosensory cortex in the parietal lobe of the cerebrum

Aneuron is the fundamental structural and functional unit of the nervous system, It is specialized to receive,
process, and transmit information using electrical and chemical signals, enabling the body to respond to internal
and external stimuli.

1. Sensory Neurons (Afferent Neurons) 2

MUSCLES AND GLANDS AS EFFECTORS

The nervous system not only detects and processes information but also produces a response to maintain
balance and ensure survival. This response is carried out by effectors — specialized structures that bring about an
action when stimulated by nerve impulses. In humans, the two main effectors are muscles and glands.

What are Effectors?

Effectors are organs or tissues that receive commands from the central nervolls system through motor
neurons and then carry out the required response. They serve as the final link in the pathway of information flow,
converting nerve impulses into visible actions such as movement, secretion, or regulation of body functions.

Muscles as Effectors

Muscles serve as the mechanical effectors of the nervous system. When stimulated by motor neurons, they
convert electrical impulses into mechanical action by contracting and generating force. This allows the body to
move, maintain posture, circulate blood, and regulate essential internal processes. Muscles are essential not only
for voluntary actions like walking or speaking but also for involuntary functions such as breathing, digestion, and
heartbeat.
> Types of Muscles as Effectors
1." Skeletal Muscles

¢ Structure and Control: Skeletal muscles are attached to bones via tendons and are under voluntary
control through the somatic nervous system. .

Glands as Effectors

Glands are specialized structures in the body that produce and secrete substances essential for regulation,

coordination, and survival. Unlike muscles, which bring about movement, glands act as chemical effectors. They
release their secretions either into ducts or directly into the bloodstream, influencing processes such as digestion,
temperature regulation, stress response, metabolism, and reproduction. Glands may be stimulated by nerve
impulses (neurogenic control) or by chemical messengers (hormonal feedback).

1.

Exocrine Glands
Exocrine glands discharge their secretions into ducts that open either onto the body surface or into body
cavities. These secretions perform local and immediate actions.

2. Endocrine Glands

Endocrine glands are ductless glands that secrete hormones directly into the bloodstream. Unlike exocrine

secretions, hormones act on distant target organs and regulate long-term and wide-ranging processes.

* Examples and Roles:

o Adrenal glands: Secrete adrenaline (epinephrine) during stress, preparing the body for “fight or
flight” by increasing heart rate, blood pressure, and glucose availability.

© Pancreas (Islets of Langerhans): Secretes insulin and glucagon, hormones essential for regulating
blood glucose levels.

o Thyroid gland: Produces thyroxine, which regulates metabolism, growth, and energy balance.

© Pituitary gland: Known as the “master gland,” it secretes hormones that control other endocrine
glands and regulate growth, reproduction, and water balance. .

¢ Application and Impact: Endocrine control is vital for maintaining homeostasis. For instance, a deficiency

of insulin leads to diabetes mellitus, while overproduction of thyroxine causes hyperthyroidism. Medical

treatment often involves replacing or regulating these hormones to restore balance.

Together, exocrine and endocrine glands ensure the body's adaptation to both internal and external
demands. For example, when running on a hot day, sweat glands (exocrine) release sweat to cool the body, while
adrenal glands (endocrine) secrete adrenaline to increase stamina and prepare muscles for exertion. This shows
how glands function as chemical executors of the nervous system’s commands.

> Real-Life Applications and Impact

* Medical Relevance: Diseases such'as myasthenia gravis (affecting skeletal muscle response) or diabetes

* Everyday Example: Sweating while running on a hot day involves both muscles (increased heart and

and causing movement, while glands respond by secreting essential substances. Together, they ensure that the
body can interact with the environment, maintain internal balance, and survive under varying conditions.

DETAILED STRUCTURE OF SENSORY, ASSOCIATIVE AND MOTOR NEURONS

Although neurons vary in their size and shape, they all share the same fundamental plan: a cell body (soma)
with a nucleus and Niss! bodies, dendrites that receive information, and an axon that conducts impulses to another
cell. Within this general plan, sensory neurons, associative (interneurons), and motor neurons each show distinct
structural features that enable them to perform their specialized functions. The following sections describe their
detailed structures, as required for annotation and examination.

Sensory Neurons (Afferent Neurons)

Sensory neurons are responsible for transmitting information from receptors in the skin, muscles, joints, and
internal organs to the central nervous system (CNS). Most vertebrate sensory neurons are pseudounipolar, a
design well suited for rapid conduction.

The cell body of a sensory neuron Is located in the dorsal root ganglion of spinal nerves or in the sensory
ganglia of cranial nerves. This soma is typically round, with a large nucleus and a prominent nucleolus, and is
encircled by satellite glial cells. A key feature of these ganglion cells is that they do not receive synapses directly

on their soma.

Associative Neurons (interneurons)

interneurons, also called relay neurons or association neurons, are located entirely within the CNS. They
form the largest class of neurons and are essential for the integration, processing, and relay of information
between sensory and motor pathways.

Structurally, interneurons are usually multipolar, with a relatively small cell body containing abundant Niss!

These dendrites receive signals from sensory neurons or other interneurons. The axon of an interneuron is
typically short and may be unmyelinated or only thinly myelinated. It gives off numerous collaterals that form
synapses locally within the grey matter of the brainor spinal cord.

This dense network of dendrites and short axons enables interneurons to establish thousands of synaptic
connections. Their structural plan perfectly matches thelr role in reflex arcs, local circuit activity, and higher brain
functions such as memory, learning, and decision-making.

In summary, interneurons are designed for integration and coordination rather than long-distance
transmission.

Motor Neurons (Efferent Neurons)

Motor neurons carry impulses from the CNS to
effector organs, such as muscles and glands, thereby
converting neural activity into action, The best-studied
example is the lower motor neuron whose cell body
lies in the anterior.(ventral) horn of the spinal cord.

Motor neurons are characteristically multipolar,
with a large polygonal cell body, a prominent central
nucleus, and coarse Niss! bodies in the cytoplasm.
From the soma emerge numerous dendrites, which
form an extensive dendritic tree capable of receiving
input from thousands of synapses.

At one side of the soma lies the axon hillock, a
Nissl-free zone that gives rise to the axon initial
segment, the site where action potentials are
generated. The axons typically very long and thickly
myelinated, ensuring rapid conduction over
considerable distances. Along its course, the axon
exhibits nodes of Ranvier, where ionic exchange occurs during saltatory conduction.

The axon eventually branches into telodendria, each ending in a synaptic bouton at a neuromuscular
junction (NMJ). Here, the motor neuron releases acetylcholine into the synaptic cleft, initiating contraction of the
skeletal muscle fiber.

This structural plan reflects the motor neuron’s essential role: to transmit fast, precise commands from the
CNS to muscles or glands, thereby enabling both voluntary and involuntary responses.

RELATING THE STRUCTURE OF NEURONS WITH THEIR FUNCTIONS

Neurons are specialized cells whose structures are perfectly adapted to their functions in the nervous

system. Each part of a neuron—cell body, dendrites, axon, and synaptic terminals—plays a distinct role in
receiving, processing, and transmitting information, The efficiency of the nervous system depends on this precise
relationship between structure and function.

--- Page 15 ---

Cell Body (Soma or Perikaryon)

The cell body serves as the metabolic and synthetic center of the neuron. It contains a large nucleus with a
prominent nucleolus, indicating high levels of gene activity. The cytoplasm, also called neuroplasm, Is rich in Niss!
bodies (aggregations of rough endoplasmic reticulum and ribosomes), which produce structural proteins and
neurotransmitters required for signal transmission.

Other organelles, such as numerous mitochondria, provide the ATP needed for ion pumping, axonal
transport, and synaptic activity. The Golgi apparatus packages proteins into vesicles for delivery to axon terminals.
A robust cytoskeleton (microtubules, actin filaments, and neurofilaments) maintains the shape of the cell and
forms tracks for axonal transport. Together, these features ensure that the soma supports the enormous
metabolic demands of the neuron and sustains long-distance communication.

Dendrites: The Receiving Structures

Dendrites are short, tapering, and highly branched extensions that provide a large surface area for synaptic
input. Many dendrites bear dendritic spines, which are small projections packed with postsynaptic receptors and
signaling molecules, These spines are sites of synaptic plasticity, meaning their structure changes during
processes like learning and memory.

Dendrites act as the receptive region of the neuron, They receive signals from thousands of presynaptic
terminals, and their branching pattern allows spatial and temporal summation of incoming impulses. In this way,
dendrites filter and integrate information before it reaches the cell body and axon hillock.

Axon: The Conducting Structure

Each neuron has a single axon, which conducts impulses away from the cell body. The axon arises from a
specialized region called the axon hillock, and its adjacent Initial segment is rich in voltage-gated sodium
channels, making it the trigger zone for action potential initiation.

The diameter of the axon strongly influences conduction speed; larger diameters reduce resistance and
conduct impulses faster. Within the axon, bundles of microtubules and neurofilaments serve as tracks for
anterograde transport (moving vesicles and organelles from the soma to terminals via kinesin) and retrograde
transport (carrying signals and waste back to the soma via.dynein).

Myelination and Saltatory Conduction

Many axons are covered by a myelin sheath, formed by Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system
(PNS) or by oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system (CNS). Myelin acts as an insulating layer, interrupted
at intervals by Nodes of Ranvier, where the axonal membrane is exposed,

This arrangement allows saltatory conduction, in which action potentials “jump” from one node to the next,
greatly increasing the speed and energy efficiency of impulse transmission. Myelinated axons make up the white
matter of the nervous system, while non-myelinated axons conduct impulses more slowly by continuous
conduction and are common in the autonomic system.

Axon Terminals (Synaptic Boutons)

At its end, the axon branches into numerous axon terminals (or synaptic boutons), Each terminal contains
synaptic vesicles, mitochondria, and specialized proteins for neurotransmitter release. When an action potential
arrives, voltage-gated calclum channels open, triggering the fusion of vesicles with the presynaptic membrane.
Neurotransmitters are then released into the synaptic cleft, allowing the conversion of an electrical signal into a
chemical signal.

Synapses: The Communication Points
Neurons communicate at specialized junctions called synapses, Most are chemical synapses, which consist
of a presynaptic terminal, a synaptic cleft, and a postsynaptic membrane rich in receptors. These synapses allow
unidirectional, modifiable, and amplifiable transmission, essential for processes such as learning and memory.
In contrast, electrical synapses (formed by gap junctions) allow direct cytoplasmic continulty between
neurons. They enable fast and often bidirectional transmission, useful for synchronization of neuronal activity.

networks that coordinate voluntary actions, such as muscle movement, and involuntary processes, such as

Q.2 How do ion concentrations differ across the membrane during the resting state?
Ans. During the resting state, the ionic composition of the intracellular and

extracellular fluids differs significantly, producing the resting | . y+ — copii seg fa

charges across the membrane is the basis of neuronal excitability.

Q7 Why is the action potential considered an “all-or-none" response?

Ans. The action potential is described as an “all-or-none" phenomenon
because it only occurs when the threshold stimulus, typically around — MARKING SCHEME (3 Marks)

ROLE OF LOCAL CIRCUITS IN SALTATORY CONDUCTION

Saltatory Conduction

In myelinated axons, nerve impulses are transmitted by a special process called saltatory conduction. in
this mode, the action potential does not travel continuously along every part of the axon membrane. Instead, it
appears to “jump” from one node of Ranvier to the next, bypassing the myelinated regions. This adaptation
makes conduction both much faster and more energy-efficient compared to unmyelinated fibres.

Mechanism: Local Circuits of lonic Current
The key mechanism underlying saltatory conduction is the formation of local circuits within the axon:

At an active node of Ranvier, voltage-gated sodium channels open, allowing a rapid influx of Na* ions,
which depolarizes that region.

These sodium ions spread longitudinally inside the axoplasm, creating a local circuit of current that
flows from thedepolarized node toward the adjacent resting node.

When this current reaches the next node, where sodium channels are densely concentrated, it
depolarizes the membrane to threshold potential.

A new action potential is then generated at that node, while the previous one enters its refractory
phase, ensuring forward movement only.

Thus, the impulse does not regenerate along the entire length of the axonal membrane, but is renewed
only at the nodes, giving the appearance of “jumping.”

Significance of Local Circuits

Local circuits make saltatory conduction possible and provide several advantages:

Rapid transmission: The action potential leaps between nodes, greatly accelerating the speed of
conduction (up to 120 m/s in humans).

Energy efficiency: Only nodal regions undergo depolarization, so fewer ions cross the membrane and
the Na*/K* pump expends less ATP.

Directional propagation: The refractory period behind each impulse prevents backward spread,
ensuring unidirectional flow of nerve signals. .

SYNAPTIC TRANSMISSION OF NERVE IMPULSE

The synapse is not just a structural junction but a functional site where the nerve impulse is transmitted
from one neuron to another, or from a neuron to an effector cell. This process, called synaptic transmission,
converts an electrical signal (action potential) in the presynaptic neuron into a chemical signal in the synaptic cleft,
and then back into an electrical response in the postsynaptic cell.

Sequence of Synaptic Transmission:

1. Arrival of Action Potential: When an action potential reaches the synaptic knob of the presynaptic neuron,
it triggers the opening of voltage-gated calcium channels in the presynaptic membrane.

2. _ Influx of Calcium Ions: Due to a steep concentration gradient, Ca** lons enter the synaptic knob, increasing
intracellular calcium levels.

3. Vesicle Fusion and Neurotransmitter Release: Elevated calcium concentration stimulates synaptic vesicles
to migrate and fuse with the presynaptic membrane. As a result, neurotransmitters are released into the
synaptic cleft via exocytosis.

4. _ Diffusion and Receptor Binding: Neurotransmitter molecules diffuse across the synaptic cleft and bind to
specific receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.

5. Generation of Postsynaptic Potential: Binding of neurotransmitters causes lon channels (e.g., Na* channels)
to open, allowing the influx of sodium Ions. This depolarizes the postsynaptic membrane and may initiate a
new action potential. If this depolarization is sufficient to reach the threshold, it is termed an Excitatory
Postsynaptic Potential (EPSP).

CLASSIFICATIONS OF NEUROTRANSMITTERS
—
Neurotransmitters are chemical messengers that mediate communication between neurons or between
neurons and effectors across synapses. Depending on their action on the postsynaptic membrane, they are
classified as excitatory or inhibitory.

’ Excitatory Neurotransmitters
Excitatory neurotransmitters increase the likelihood of generating an action potential in the postsynaptic
neuron. They achieve this by enhancing the membrane’s permeability to sodium ions (Na*), thereby depolarizing
the membrane and bringing it closer to the threshold potential.
A prominent example is acetylcholine (ACh), which acts as a key excitatory neurotransmitter in the
peripheral nervous system, particularly at neuromuscular junctions. In the central nervous system (CNS), several
biogenic amines (derived from amino acids) serve excitatory roles.

CRANIAL AND SPINAL NERVES IN MAN

The peripheral nervous system (PNS) consists of cranial and spinal nerves, which connect the central

nervous system (CNS)—the brain and spinal cord—to the rest of the body. These nerves transmit sensory
information to the CNS and motor commands from the CNS to muscles and glands, enabling voluntary and
involuntary responses. Architecturally, the nerves are bundles of axons (nerve fibers) enclosed in connective
tissue sheaths, allowing rapid signal conduction.

Cranial Nerves
Humans have 12 pairs of cranial nerves that arise directly from the brain (primarily the brainstem) rather
than the spinal cord. Each nerve is specialized for specific sensory, motor, or mixed functions:

1. Olfactory (!)— sensory; responsible for smell.

2. Optic (i!) sensory; carries visual information from the retina to the brain.

3. Oculomotor (lif) — motor; controls most eye movements and pupil constriction.

4. Trochlear (IV) = motor; innervates the superior oblique muscle of the eye.

5. Trigeminal (V) — mixed; provides sensory input from the face and motor control for mastication.

9. Glossopharyngeal (IX) — mixed; involved in taste, swallowing, and salivary secretion.

11. Accessory (XI) — motor; controls neck and shoulder muscles.

12. Hypoglossal (XII) — motor; manages tongue movements.

Cranial nerves are structurally distinct in that some are purely sensory, purely motor, or mixed, depending
on whether they carry afferent (sensory) or efferent (motor) fibers. They emerge from the brainstem or
forebrain, pass through cranial foramina, and branch extensively to reach their target organs.

Humans have 31 pairs of spinal nerves, each arising from the spinal cord via a dorsal (posterior) root and a
ventral (anterior) root. These nerves are mixed, carrying both sensory fibers (from the dorsal root ganglia)
and motor fibers (from ventral horn neurons).

Coccygeal (1 pair) — tailbone area.

After emerging from the vertebral column, spinal nerves branch into dorsal rami, which innervate back

STRUCTURE TYPES AND FUNCTIONS OF THE AUTONOMIC NERVOUS SYSTEM

Peripheral Nervous System: A Brief Overview
The peripheral nervous system (PNS) links the central nervous system (CNS) with the rest of the body. It is
divided into two main parts:
1. The somatic nervous system, which controls voluntary activities through skeletal muscles.
2. The autonomic nervous system (ANS), which regulates involuntary functions of internal organs, smooth
muscles, and glands.
> Structure of the Autonomic Nervous System
The autonomic nervous system operates largely beyond conscious control and ensures the maintenance of
homeostasis. Structurally, it consists of motor pathways with two neurons in sequence:
© Apreganglionic neuron, whose cell body lies in the CNS and whose axon extends to a ganglion.
* A postganglionic neuron, which arises from the ganglion and innervates the target organ (smooth
muscle, cardiac muscle, or gland).
Autonomic ganglia serve as relay centers, while autonomic nerves are distributed widely to thoracic,
abdominal, and pelvic organs, as well as blood vessels and glands.

> Types of Autonomic Nervous System
The autonomic nervous system (ANS) has two major divisions: the sympathetic and the parasympathetic
Nervous systems. Though they often act in opposite ways, they work together to maintain homeostasis,
adjusting the body's functions according to stress, rest, or energy demands.
1. Sympathetic Nervous System (SNS)
The sympathetic division is sometimes referred to as the thoracolumbar outflow, because its preganglionic
neurons arise from the thoracic (T1-T12) and lumbar (L1-12) regions of the spinal cord.
Structural Features:
*  Preganglionic fibers are short and synapse in sympathetic ganglia located near the spinal cord
(paravertebral or prevertebral ganglia).
* Postganglionic fibers are long, enabling the sympathetic system to reach many organs simultaneously,
producing a rapid, body-wide response.
© Neurotransmitters: Noradrenaline (norepinephrine) is the main transmitter at effector organs (except
sweat glands, which use acetylcholine).
Functions:
The SNS prepares the body for “fight or flight” situations—sudden stress, danger, or intense physical
activity. Its effects include:

The sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions are antagonistic yet complementary. For example:

* During exercise, the sympathetic system raises heart rate and suppresses digestion.

* Afterward, the parasympathetic system slows the heart rate and restores digestive activity.

This balance ensures the body can adapt to both sudden challenges and periods of rest.

The sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions usually act in antagonistic yet complementary ways,
