Respiration, like all essential life processes, operates at two distinct levels: the cellular fevel and the
organismic level. At the cellular level, the process is termed internal respiration. It is a catabolic reaction involving
the enzymatic breakdown of complex organic compounds, such as glucose, into simpler molecules—primarily
carbon dioxide and water—accompanied by the release of energy in the form of ATP (adenosine triphosphate).
This energy fuels various metabolic activities essential for life.

At the organismic level, the process is known as external respiration, which involves the exchange of gases
between the organism and its environment. Oxygen is inhaled into the body, transported to tissues, and carbon
dioxide—produced as a byproduct of internal respiration—is expelled.

These two levels of respiration are intricately linked. The oxygen taken in during external respiration serves
as the final electron acceptor in cellular respiration, while the carbon dioxide generated at the cellular level is
removed through the process of breathing.

This chapter explores the biological, biochemical, and physiological dimensions of respiration, highlighting
the mechanisms, adaptations, and significance of this fundamental life process.

Definition

The respiratory surface is the specific region of an organism where the actual exchange of gases with the
exteinal environment takes place. This surface plays a central role in facilitating the diffusion of respiratory
gases—primarily oxygen and carbon dioxide—between the organism and its surroundings.

Properties of Respiratory Surface

For gas exchange to be efficient, the respiratory surface must exhibit certain critical properties:

¢ Moist and Permeable: Gases dissolve in moisture before diffusing across membranes. A moist surface
ensures that oxygenand'carbon dioxide can effectively pass through the membrane.

¢ Thin Structure: Diffusion is only efficient over short distances. A thin respiratory membrane (generally
less than 1 mm) minimizes the path length for gas molecules, enhancing the rate of exchange.

Lobes of the Lungs
The human lungs are divided into distinct lobes, each separated by deep fissures. These lobes ensure
efficient distribution of air and blood within the lungs, allowing for optimal gas exchange.

Right Lung

>

>

The right lung is larger in volume (about 55% of total lung capacity) but shorter and broader because
the diaphragm rises higher on the right side due to the underlying liver.

It is divided into three lobes:

1. Superior lobe — located at the apex and anterior region.

2. Middle lobe — wedge-shaped, situated between the other two lobes.

3. Inferior lobe — the largest, extending toward the base of the lung.

These lobes are separated by two fissures:

o Horizontal fissure — separates the superior lobe from the middle lobe.

o Oblique fissure —runs diagonally, separating the inferior lobe from both the superior and middle lobes.

Left Lung

The left lung is narrower and slightly longer because it shares space with the heart within the thoracic cavity.

It comprises two lobes:

1. Superior lobe — forms the upper part and contains the lingula, a tongue-like projection that partly
corresponds to the middle lobe of the right lung.

2. Inferior lobe — occupies the base.

The lobes are separated by a single oblique fissure.

A distinctive feature is the cardiac notch, an indentation on the anterior border that accommodates the

apex of the heart.

Pleural Covering
The lungs are delicate and spongy organs, and to protect them, they are enclosed within a specialized
double-layered membrane known as the pleura. This pleural covering plays an essential role in both protection
and the mechanics of breathing.
1. Layers of the Pleura
e Visceral pleura: This inner layer is firmly attached to the external surface of the lung, covering every
lobe and fissure. It moves with the lung tissue during all respiratory movements.
¢ Parietal pleura: This outer layer lines the thoracic cavity, the upper surface of the diaphragm, and the
sides of the mediastinum, It acts as a boundary between the lungs and the walls of the chest cavity.
2. Pleural Cavity
© The space between the visceral and parietal layers is called the pleural cavity.
© Itis not an empty space but a potential space containing a very thin film of pleural fluid secreted by the
pleura.
3. Functions of Pleural Fluid’
© Lubrication: The pleural fluid minimizes friction between the two pleural layers during inhalation and
exhalation, allowing smooth and pain-free movement of the lungs.
© Surface tension: The fluid creates a slight adhesive force that helps the lungs remain in close contact
with the thoracic wall. This ensures that when the chest cavity expands during inspiration, the lungs
expand along with it.
4. — Clinical Importance
e Disturbances of the pleural cavity can lead to respiratory problems. For example:
© Pleurisy: Inflammation of the pleura, causing painful breathing.
o Pleural effusion: Excess accumulation of fluid in the pleural cavity, restricting lung expansion.
© Pneumothorax: Entry of air into the pleural cavity, which can cause lung collapse.

VENTILATION MECHANISM IN HUMANS

Ventilation (also called breathing) is the mechanical process by which air is moved into and out of the lungs,
ensuring a continuous supply of oxygen and removal of carbon dioxide. It occurs in two phases: inhalation
(inspiration) and exhalation (expiration).

1. Inhalation (Inspiration)
Inhalation is the process by which air enters the lungs. It is considered an active phase of breathing, because
it requires the contraction of respiratory muscles and the expenditure of energy. This process ensures that
oxygen-rich air is drawn into the alveoli, where gas exchange takes place.

Pressure Relationships in Ventilation
Breathing (ventilation) depends upon the creation of pressure gradients between the atmosphere, alveoli,
and pleural cavity. Air moves into or out of the lungs by bulk flow, always following the rule:
“Air flows from a region of higher pressure to a region of lower pressure.”
To understand this, three major pressures are important:
1, Atmospheric Pressure (Patm)
© This is the pressure exerted by the air surrounding the body.
© Atsea level, it is approximately 760 mmHg (millimeters of mercury).
© Atmospheric pressure is considered the reference point against which other pressures are
measured.
2. Intrapulmonary (Alveolar) Pressure (Palv)
© This is the pressure inside the alveoli of the lungs.
© It changes continuously during breathing:
* - During inhalation: Palv falls slightly below Patm (about —1 mmHg), drawing air into the lungs.
* During exhalation: Palv rises slightly above Patm (about +1 mmHg), pushing air out of the lungs.
o Atthe end of each breath, when there is no airflow, Palv becomes equal to Patm.
3. Intrapleural Pressure (Pip)
© This is the pressure inside the pleural cavity, between the visceral and parietal pleura.
© It is always slightly negative compared to both Patm and Palv, usually around -4 mmHg (= 756
mmHg) at rest.
© The negative pressure is essential because it:
"Prevents lung collapse.
* Keeps the lungs closely attached to the thoracic wall, so they expand when the chest expands,
Importance of Pressure Gradients
© Inhalation: Thoracic cavity expands —> Pip becomes more negative — Palv falls below Patm — air flows
into the lungs.
Exhalation: Thoracic cavity reduces in size —-> Pip becomes less negative — Palv rises above Patm > air
flows out of the lungs.
In summary, ventilation is governed by the relationship among atmospheric, intrapulmonary, and intrapleural
pressures. These dynamic pressure changes ensure that air moves in and out of the lungs efficiently.

ROLE OF RESPIRATORY PIGMENTS

Respiratory pigments are coloured molecules that play a vital role in the transport of oxygen within
organisms. These pigments bind reversibly with oxygen, allowing them to pick up oxygen in regions of high
concentration (e.g., lungs or gills) and release it where it is needed (e.g., tissues undergoing respiration). A
common feature among all respiratory pigments is the presence of a coloured, non-protein component—for
example, the haem group in haemoglobin.

Two of the most studied respiratory pigments in vertebrates are haemoglobin and myoglobin.
Haemoglobin

Haemoglobin is a large, globular protein found within red blood cells. It is composed of :our polypeptide
chains, each associated with a haem group that contains a central iron (Fe**) atom. This iron atom is capable of
binding one molecule of oxygen, allowing each haemoglobin molecule to carry up to four oxygen molecules.

Oxygen binding to haemoglobin is reversible and depends on the partial pressure of oxygen (pO,). In

oxygen-rich environments such as the alveoli of the lungs, haemoglobin readily binds oxygen to form
oxyhaemoglobin. In tissues with low pO2, oxygen is released to support cellular respiration.

Myoglobin

Myoglobin is a structurally simpler pigment found in skeletal and cardiac muscles. It consists of a single
polypeptide chain and a single haem group, allowing it to bind one molecule of oxygen. Myoglobin has a higher
affinity for oxygen than haemoglobin and serves as an intracellular oxygen reservoir.

This pigment becomes particularly important when oxygen levels drop below 20 mmHg—a condition that
may occur during intense muscular activity. At such low oxygen pressures, myoglobin releases its stored oxygen,
thereby sustaining aerobic respiration in muscle cells when haemoglobin-derived oxygen becomes limited. It is

also responsible for the red colour of muscle tissue.

EFFECTS OF SMOKING ON THE RESPIRATORY SYSTEM

Smoking is one of the most harmful lifestyle habits and a leading cause of respiratory illness worldwide.

Tobacco smoke contains thousands of toxic substances such as nicotine, tar, and carbon monoxide, along with
many cancer-causing chemicals. These substances damage the air passages, alveoli, and protective mechanisms
of the lungs, producing both short-term irritation and long-term chronic diseases.

>»

Irritation and Inflammation of Airways

When a person inhales cigarette smoke, the chemicals
irritate the lining of the trachea and bronchi. This
leads to swelling and inflammation of the airways,
accompanied by excessive mucus secretion. Over
time, this constant irritation causes a persistent
cough, often referred to as a “smoker's cough,” which
is one of the earliest warning signs of lung damage.
Paralysis of Cilia

The respiratory tract is normally lined with tiny hair-
like structures called cilia, which move rhythmically to
sweep out dust, microbes, and mucus. Smoking a
paralyzes or even destroys these cilia. As a result,

harmful particles remain trapped in the lungs instead
of being cleared out. This greatly increases the risk of
respiratory infections such as bronchitis and =
pneumonia, since the lungs lose their natural defense
system.

> Chronic Bronchitis

With long-term smoking, the bronchi remain chronically inflamed and produce large amounts of thick
mucus. The narrowed and clogged airways make breathing difficult and cause constant coughing. This
condition, known as chronic bronchitis, is commonly seen in long-term smokers who experience frequent
chest infections and reduced lung efficiency.

> Emphysema

One of the most serious effects of smoking is emphysema, in which the walls of the alveoli (air sacs) are
progressively destroyed. Instead of many small alveoli providing a large surface area for gas exchange, the
alveoli fuse into larger, less efficient sacs. This reduces the surface area for oxygen absorption, leading to
severe shortness of breath. Patients with emphysema often develop a “barrel-shaped chest” and may
become dependent on supplemental oxygen in advanced stages.

>» Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD)

Chronic bronchitis and emphysema together are referred to as Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease
(COPD). Smoking is the leading cause of COPD worldwide. It is a progressive disease in which airflow
becomes permanently obstructed, making exhalation difficult. Patients with COPD suffer from constant
breathlessness, wheezing, and greatly reduced exercise tolerance.

> Lung Cancer

Smoking is the major cause of lung cancer, responsible for over 80% of cases globally. Carcinogens present

in tobacco smoke damage the DNA of lung cells, leading to uncontrolled cell growth. Lung cancer is

particularly dangerous because symptoms often appear late, when the disease has already spread. This
makes survival rates very low compared to many other cancers.
>» Reduced Oxygen Transport

Another harmful effect of smoking is reduced oxygen transport in the blood. Carbon monoxide, a poisonous

gas in cigarette smoke, binds to hemoglobin with much greater affinity than oxygen. This prevents

hemoglobin from carrying oxygen efficiently, depriving tissues of oxygen. Smokers may experience fatigue,
dizziness, and a higher risk of heart and brain complications due to reduced oxygen supply.
> Increased Susceptibility to Infections

Since smoking damages both the cilia and the immune defenses of the respiratory system, smokers are far

more prone to infections such as tuberculosis, influenza, and pneumonia. Healing of damaged respiratory

tissues is also slower in smokers, further worsening recovery from illness.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), tobacco use kills more than 8 million people every year,
with respiratory diseases being a major cause of death. In countries like Pakistan, smoking-related illnesses such
as COPD, lung cancer, and tuberculosis place a heavy burden on public health systems. Many of these conditions
are preventable if smoking is avoided or stopped early in life.

Smoking affects the respiratory system by irritating and inflaming the airways, paralyzing cilia, causing
chronic bronchitis, damaging alveoli in emphysema, leading to COPD, and greatly increasing the risk of lung cancer.
It also reduces oxygen transport due to carbon monoxide and makes the body more vulnerable to infections.
