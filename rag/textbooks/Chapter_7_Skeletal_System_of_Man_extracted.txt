Support is a fundamental requirement for all living organisms. It is essential to maintain body shape, resist
the pull of gravity, and withstand various external forces. As organisms evolved and increased in size, their
structural demands became more complex. This need for support became particularly critical when animals
transitioned from aquatic to terrestrial environments, where buoyant forces no longer assisted in maintaining
body structure. In animals, this vital supportive function is primarily carried out by the skeleton, which provides a
rigid framework for maintaining posture, facilitating movement, and protecting internal organs.

The human skeletal system, or human skeleton, is a highly organized structure composed of bones and
cartilage. Bones form the primary rigid framework, while cartilage provides flexible support at specific points such
as joints. Together, these components perform several essential functions. They provide structural support to the
body’s soft tissues, enabling posture and stability. They facilitate movement by serving as attachment sites for
skeletal muscles, which act across joints to produce coordinated motion. In addition to support and locomotion,
the skeletal system protects vital organs, stores essential minerals such as calcium and phosphorus, and houses
bone marrow, which is.responsible for the formation of blood cells.

Thus, the human skeleton is not merely a static framework but a dynamic system integral to movement,
protection, and overall physiological function, reflecting the remarkable adaptability and complexity of the human

body.

1. Structure of Bone
Bone is a highly specialized, rigid connective tissue that forms the primary component of the human
skeleton. It provides structural support, facilitates movement, protects vital organs, stores minerals, and
houses bone marrow. Bone tissue exhibits both strength and resilience due to its unique composition and
organization,
The human bone is a remarkable composite material, combining both organic and inorganic components to
achieve a balance of strength, flexibility, and resilience. This unique composition allows bones to withstand
mechanical stresses while maintaining a lightweight structure essential for movement.
>» Composition of Bone
(a) Organic Component (=30-35%)

The organic portion of bone is primarily responsible for its flexibility and tensile strength, preventing

bones from being brittle under stress. It consists of:

© Collagen fibers: Type |.collagen is the dominant protein in bone. These long, fibrous molecules form
a dense, interwoven network that provides tensile strength, allowing bones to resist stretching and
bending forces. Collagen fibers also serve as a scaffold for the deposition of minerals during bone
formation.

© Non-collagenous proteins: These include osteocalcin, osteonectin, and bone sialoprotein. They play
critical roles in regulating mineralization, binding calcium ions, and facilitating the proper
organization of hydroxyapatite crystals within the bone matrix. Additionally, they contribute to bone
repair and remodeling, ensuring that micro-damage from daily stress is continually repaired.

© Proteoglycans and glycoproteins: These molecules, though present in smaller amounts, retain water
within the bone matrix, adding slight compressive resilience and contributing to the elasticity of bone.

(b) Inorganic Component (=65-70%)

The inorganic fraction is responsible for the hardness and compressive strength of bone. It consists

mainly of:

© Hydroxyapatite [Ca:o(PO.)s(OH)2]: This calcium phosphate mineral is deposited in and around the
collagen framework. Hydroxyapatite crystals form a rigid lattice that makes bones hard and capable
of supporting body weight and resisting mechanical pressure.

o Other mineral ions: Small amounts of magnesium, sodium, carbonate, and fluoride ions are also
incorporated into the bone matrix. These ions influence the érystallinity, solubility, and overall
strength of the mineralized bone, and help in maintaining mineral homeostasis in the body.

© Function of inorganic components: By providing rigidity, the inorganic component allows bones to
act as structural supports and levers for muscle action. The mineralized matrix also serves as a
reservoir for essential ions like calcium and phosphate, which can be mobilized when needed for
physiological functions such as nerve conduction, muscle contraction, and blood clotting.

> Integration of Organic and Inorganic Components:
The interplay between the organic collagen matrix and the inorganic hydroxyapatite crystals gives bone its
unique combination of strength and toughness. Collagen fibers absorb tension, while hydroxyapatite resists
compression. This composite nature ensures that bone is neither too brittle (like pure mineral) nor too
flexible (like cartilage), making it ideal for the mechanical demands of the human body.

» Microscopic Structure of Bone
Bone is not a homogeneous tissue but exhibits a highly organized microscopic architecture that enables it to
fulfill multiple structural and physiological functions. At the microscopic level, bone can be broadly

categorized into compact (cortical) bone and spongy (cancellous or trabecular) bone, each with distinct
structural features and functional significance.

(a) Compact (Cortical) Bone

Compact bone forms the dense, outer layer of bones, providing strength and rigidity. It is most

prominent in the diaphysis (shaft) of long bones. The key structural unit of compact bone is the osteon,

also known as the Haversian system.

© Osteons: Each osteon consists of a central Haversian canal that contains blood vessels, nerves, and
lymphatic vessels, ensuring the supply of nutrients and removal of metabolic waste. Surrounding
the canal are concentric layers of mineralized matrix called lamellae, which are organized in rings to
resist torsional and compressive forces.

©° Osteocytes and lacunae: Osteocytes, the mature bone cells, are embedded within small cavities
called lacunae situated between the lamellae, These cells maintain the bone matrix and
communicate with each other through fine, microscopic channels known as canaliculi. Canaliculi
form an extensive network that allows the exchange of nutrients, gases, and metabolic wastes
between osteocytes and the blood vessels in the Haversian canals.

© Interconnection: Osteons are interconnected by Volkmann’s canals, which run perpendicular to the
Haversian canals, linking adjacent osteons and integrating the vascular network throughout the
compact bone. This organization ensures that compact bone is highly resilient and capable of
bearing significant mechanical loads.

{b) Spongy (Cancellous or Trabecular) Bone

Spongy bone is found primarily at the epiphyses of long bones, within flat bones, and surrounding the

medullary cavity. Unlike compact bone, it is less dense and has a porous, lattice-like arrangement that

maximizes strength while'minimizing weight.

© Trabeculae: The porous structure consists of thin plates and rods called trabeculae, which are
oriented along lines of mechanical stress. This arrangement allows spongy bone to resist
multidirectional forces efficiently while keeping the bone lightweight.

© Bone marrow spaces: The spaces between trabeculae are filled with red bone marrow, the site of
hematopoiesis (formation of red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets). This feature makes
spongy bone crucial not only for structural support but also for maintaining vital physiological
processes.

© Functional advantage: The trabecular architecture also provides slight flexibility, reducing the risk
of fractures by absorbing shocks and distributing stress throughout the bone.

Structural Parts and Internal Composition of Long Bone

Structurally, a long bone consists of two expanded ends. epiphyses and a central shaft known as the
diaphysis. The diaphysis encloses a medullary cavity, is usually filled with yellow bone marrow, a
tissue primarily involved in fat storage.

Periosteum: The outer surface of the bone is enveloped by the periosteum, a tough fibrous membrane
that serves as an anchoring site for tendons and ligaments. It contains blood vessels, lymphatic vessels,
and nerves, all of which contribute to the nourishment and repair of bone.

Endosteum: The inner surfaces of bone, including the medullary cavity and spaces within spongy bone,
are lined by the endosteum, a thin vascular membrane rich in osteogenic Cells, which plays a key role in
bone growth, repair, and remodeling.

This gross structure not only reflects the mechanical strength of bone but also its adaptability and role in
energy storage and blood cell formation.

Cellular Components of Bone
Bone is a living tissue composed of specialized cells that work in a coordinated manner to maintain its
structure, enable growth, and regulate mineral balance. The cellular components of bone can be classified
into three main types: osteoblasts, osteocytes, and osteoclasts. Each plays a distinct and crucial role in bone
physiology.
(a) Osteoblasts —- The Bone-Forming Cells
Osteoblasts are mononuclear, cuboidal cells located on the surface of developing or remodeling bone.
They are primarily responsible for the synthesis of the organic bone matrix, known as osteoid, which is
rich in collagen fibers (mainly type |) and non-collagenous proteins. Osteoblasts initiate the process of
mineralization, depositing hydroxyapatite crystals within the osteoid to harden the bone.
Functions of Osteoblasts:
© Formation of new bone tissue during growth, repair, and remodeling.
oO Secretion of signaling molecules that regulate osteoclast activity and overall bone homeostasis,
© Participation in hormonal regulation of calcium and phosphate metabolism.
(b) Osteocytes ~The Mature Bone Cells
Osteocytes are former osteoblasts that have become embedded within the mineralized bone matrix.
They reside in small spaces called lacunae and extend long cytoplasmic processes through canaliculi,
forming an extensive intercellular network.
Functions of Osteocytes:
© Maintenance of ‘bone tissue by regulating the deposition and resorption of minerals in their
immediate environment.
© Sensing mechanical stress and strain on bone, thereby directing remodeling to strengthen areas
experiencing higher loads.
© Communiestisn with osteoblasts @nd osteoclasts to coordinate bone formation and resorption.
(c) Osteoclasts — The Bone-Resorbing Cells
Osteoclasts are large, multinucleated cells derived from the fusion of monocyte/macrophage
precursors. They are primarily involved in bone resorption, a process in which the mineralized matrix is
broken down, releasing calcium and phosphate ions into the bloodstream.
Functions of Osteoclasts:

© Remodeling of bone to adapt to mechanical stress and repair micro-damage.

o Regulation of blood calcium and phosphate levels, essential for physiological functions such as nerve
conduction and muscle contraction.

o Working in tandem with osteoblasts to maintain a dynamic balance between bone formation and

Structure of Cartilage
Cartilage is a specialized form of connective tissue that
combines strength with flexibility. It provides support to
various body parts, maintains structural integrity, and ensures
smooth movements at joints by reducing friction and absorbing
mechanical shocks. Unlike bone, cartilage is avascular—it lacks
blood vessels, lymphatics, and nerves. Instead, nutrients and
oxygen diffuse through the matrix from surrounding tissues.
This limited nutrient supply makes cartilage growth and repair
much slower than bone, contributing to the persistence of
cartilage injuries.
Composition of Cartilage
Cartilage is composed of cells and extracellular matrix, both of
which are adapted for its supportive and cushioning roles.

within small spaces called lacunae inside the matrix. Chondrocytes are responsible for maintaining
the cartilage matrix, regulating its turnover, and ensuring structural stability.
2. Extracellular Matrix:
© Fibrous component: Rich in type II Collagen fibers, which provide tensile strength and prevent

toughness.
© Ground substance: Contains proteoglycans (such as aggrecan) and glycosaminoglycans (like
chondroitin sulfate and hyaluronic acid), which trap large amounts of water. This hydrated gel-like
consistency makes cartilage resistant to compression and ideal for absorbing shock.
© Water content: Cartilage matrix is up to 80% water, allowing it to be resilient, elastic, and
compressible. The high water content also facilitates the diffusion of nutrients and metabolic wastes.

Q.4 Why does cartilage heal more slowly than bone?

Ans, Cartilage heals slowly because it is avascular, meaning it lacks direct blood vessels. Instead, nutrients and
oxygen must diffuse through the dense matrix to reach chondrocytes (cartilage cells). This diffusion is slow
and limits the rate of cell division and repair. In contrast, bone is richly supplied with blood vessels, allowing
rapid delivery of nutrients, growth factors, and immune cells for healing. As a result, fractured bones may
heal in weeks to months, whereas cartilage injuries, such as those in joints, often take years or remain
permanent.

Techniques for Joint Replacement (Arthroplasty)

Joint replacement, medically termed arthroplasty, involves replacing damaged or diseased
joints with artificial components. A wide range of joints, including the hip, knee, shoulder, and
elbow, can be surgically replaced. The artificial joints are typically composed of metals such as
stainless steel or titanium alloys, combined with modern polymers like high-density polyethylene,
silastic, or elastomer.

There are two main techniques used in joint replacement surgery:

* Partial Joint Replacement (Hemi-replacement Technique): in this procedure, the damaged
articular surface of the bone on only one side of the joint is removed and replaced with an
artificial component.

© Total Joint Replacement Technique: In this method, the articular surfaces on both sides of
the joint are removed and replaced with prosthetic components to restore full function.

The prosthetic components are typically affixed to the bone using a synthetic adhesive, most
commonly methyl methacrylate, to ensure secure attachment.

Relationship Between Bipedal Posture and the Human Skeleton and Musculature

muscular systems:

COMMON DISORDERS OF THE HUMAN SKELETON

The human skeletal system, though highly resilient, is susceptible to a variety of disorders that impair
movement, posture, and overall health. These disorders may arise from aging, injury, poor posture, degenerative
changes, nutritional deficiencies, or autoimmune conditions. Important disorders are discussed below:

1. Slipped Disc (Herniated Disc)
Each intervertebral disc is a cushion-like structure located between adjacent vertebrae. It is composed of
two distinct parts:
* Nucleus pulposus: a soft, gelatinous central region that provides elasticity and compressibility.
¢ Annulus fibrosus: a tough outer ring of fibrocartilage that firmly anchors the vertebrae together.
These discs act as shock absorbers during movement. A slipped disc (more accurately called a herniated
disc) occurs when the annulus fibrosus ruptures, allowing the nucleus pulposus to protrude outward. This
herniation may compress the spinal cord or nearby spinal nerves, leading to severe back pain, nerve
irritation, numbness, or even loss of motor function.
Note: The term “slipped disc” is misleading, since the disc itself does not slip out of place; only its inner
contents bulge abnormally.

2. Spondylosis
Spondylosis is a degenerative disorder of the vertebral column that results in stiffness, pain, and reduced
mobility. It is associated with the gradual wear and tear of intervertebral discs, ligaments, and vertebral
Joints. In advanced stages, it may lead to immobility and even fusion of vertebrae.
* Cervical spondylosis is a common type, involving degeneration of cervical vertebrae. It often results in
neck pain, stiffness, headaches, and sometimes compression of spinal nerves.
© — Risk factors include aging, repetitive strain, and Jong-term poor posture.

4. Arthritis

Arthritis is a general term for inflammation of joints, leading to pain, stiffness, swelling, and difficulty in

mobility. It can significantly impair everyday activities such as walking, climbing stairs, or standing up from a

seated position.

Major Types Include:

Osteoarthritis: The most common type, caused by progressive degeneration of articular cartilage. It
typically affects weight-bearing joints such as the knees, hips, and spine, producing pain after movement
and a “grating” sound in joints. :

© Rheumatoid arthritis: An autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial
membrane, causing swelling, deformity, and chronic pain.

© Gouty arthritis: Caused by excessive uric acid in the blood. Crystals of sodium urate deposit in joints,
especially the big toe, leading to sudden episodes of severe pain, redness, and swelling.

5. Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is a skeletal disorder characterized by low bone mass, increased bone fragility, and a high risk
of fractures. Bones become porous and brittle, particularly in the spine, hip, and wrist.
® Why it occurs:
© Bone mass normally peaks between ages 20-25 years.
© With advancing age—especially after menopause in women—bone resorption exceeds bone deposition.
o This imbalance gradually weakens the bones.
© Risk factors: Aging, deficiency of calcium and vitamin D, hormonal changes (low estrogen in women),
sedentary lifestyle, and prolonged use of corticosteroid medications.
* Prevention:
© Ensuring adequate calcium and vitamin D intake during childhood and adolescence, when bone
mass is being built.
o Engaging in weight-bearing exercises to strengthen bones.
o Attaining a high peak bone mass in early adulthood helps tolerate natural age-related bone loss
later in life.

preventive care. Early diagnosis and treatment can reduce the severity of these conditions and improve
quality of life.

ULTRASTRUCTURE OF SKELETAL MUSCLES

Skeletal muscles are the organs of voluntary movement. They are highly organized structures, both at the
macroscopic and microscopic levels, enabling them to generate précise and powerful contractions.

Externally, a skeletal muscle is wrapped in a connective tissue sheath called the epimysium. Each muscle is
composed of multiple bundles known as fascicles, each fascicle being surrounded by perimysium. Within a
fascicle, individual muscle fibers (muscle cells) are elongated, cylindrical, multinucleated cells. Every muscle fiber
is encased in a thin connective tissue covering called the endomysium. This hierarchical arrangement provides
both structural strength and efficient force transmission during contraction.

Ultrastructure of Skeletal Muscle

1. Muscle Fiber and Sarcolemma
Each muscle fiber is bounded by the sarcolemma, a specialized plasma membrane that not only encloses
the cell but also plays a crucial role in impulse conduction. The sarcolemma folds inward to form transverse
tubules (T-tubules), which carry electrical impulses deep into the cell, ensuring synchronized contraction of
the entire fiber. The cytoplasm, known as sarcoplasm, contains abundant mitochondria, glycogen, and the
oxygen-binding pigment myoglobin, all of which support the high energy demands of contraction.

2. Myofibrils and Sarcomeres
The bulk of the sarcoplasm is occupied by myofibrils, which are long, thread-like contractile elements
running the length of the muscle fiber. Myofibrils are composed of even smaller protein filaments, termed
myofilaments, of two main types: ; .
* Thin filaments (“7-8 nm): Composed of actin, tropomyosin, and troponin.
* Thick filaments (~16 nim): Composed primarily of myosin,
Myofibrils are organized into repeating units called sarcomeres, the fundamental contractile units of skeletal

and cardiac muscle. Each sarcomere is bounded by 2-lines. The specific arrangement of thin and thick
filaments produces the characteristic striations seen in skeletal muscle:

MUSCLE CONTRACTION - SLIDING FILAMENT MODEL

The sliding filament model is the widely accepted explanation of how skeletal muscle contracts. It was
independently proposed in the 1950s by Hugh Huxley and Jean Hanson, and it remains fundamental to modern
muscle physiology. According to this model, muscle contraction does not involve shortening of individual
filaments, but rather a sliding of thin filaments (actin) past thick filaments (myosin) within the sarcomere. This
sliding action shortens the sarcOmere and thereby the entire muscle fiber, producing contraction.

Sequence of Events in the Sliding Filament Model
1. Resting State
® Ina relaxed muscle, thin and thick filaments overlap only partially.
* Tropomyosin covers the myosin-binding sites on actin, preventing cross-bridge formation.
2. Stimulation and Calcium Release
© Anerve impulse triggers the release of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction,
* This generates an action potential that travels along the sarcolemma and into the muscle fiber via T-
tubules.
* The action potential stimulates the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) to release calcium ions (Ca**) into the
sarcoplasm.
3. Binding of Calcium to Troponin
* Calcium ions bind to troponin C (TnC) on the thin filaments.
* This induces a conformational change in the troponin complex, which shifts tropomyosin away from the
active sites on actin.
* The myosin-binding sites on actin are now exposed.
Cross-Bridge Formation
* Energized myosin heads (containing ADP and inorganic phosphate) attach to the exposed sites on actin,
forming cross-bridges.
5. Power Stroke
«The myosin head pivots, pulling the thin filament toward the centre of the sarcomere (M line).
* This action is powered by the energy released from ATP hydrolysis.
ADP and phosphate are released from the myosin head at the end of the stroke.
6. Detachment of Cross-Bridge
* Anew molecule of ATP binds to the myosin head.
© This causes the cross-bridge to detach from actin.

ANTAGONISTIC ARRANGEMENT OF SKELETAL MUSCLES

Bones are joined together by strong connective tissues called ligaments, which stabilize the joints. Skeletal
muscles are attached to bones at two points: the origin and the insertion. The origin is the fixed attachment point,
while the insertion is the movable attachment, which is drawn toward the origin when the muscle contracts. The
thick, central part of the muscle between origin and insertion is known as the belly, and it is this region that
contracts during movement.

Muscles act on bones to produce movement by crossing joints. When a flexor muscle contracts, it bends the
joint (e.g., bending the elbow or knee), whereas an extensor muscle straightens the joint. These muscles typically
work in antagonistic pairs, where the contraction of one muscle (e.g., flexor) is accompanied by the relaxation of
its counterpart (e.g., extensor), allowing controlled, bidirectional movement.

Movement in knee joint

tibia (shin bone). It allows flexion and extension, as well as limited rolling and gliding movements.

tendon divides and attaches to the upper parts of the tibia and fibula.

* Extension of the knee is carried out by the quadriceps femoris, a group of extensor muscles situated at
the front of the thigh. These muscles originate from the ilium and femur, converge around the patella
(kneecap), and insert into the tibia. They are crucial for walking, running, standing, and maintaining

(h) Rheumatoid arthritis and Gouty arthritis

Ans. Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is an autoimmune disorder in which the
body’s immune system attacks the synovial membrane, causing chronic
inflammation. Symptoms include joint stiffness, swelling, deformity,
and fatigue, often affecting multiple small joints of the hands and feet.
It is systemic and may involve other organs. In contrast, gouty arthritis is caused by accumulation of uric
acid crystals in joints. It is characterized by sudden, severe pain, redness, swelling, and warmth, commonly
in the big toe, knees, or ankles. Unlike RA, it is usually localized and not systemic. While RA is long-term and
progressive, gout often occurs in acute attacks triggered by diet, metabolism, or kidney inefficiency.

(i) Simple fracture and Compound fracture

Ans. A simple fracture refers to a bone break in which the bone does not
pierce the skin. It remains an internal injury, with minimal risk of
infection, and is usually treated by immobilization with a cast or splint
to allow natural healing. In contrast, a compound fracture involves a
bone break where the broken end protrudes through the skin. This creates an open wound, visible
externally, and carries a high risk of infection due to exposure to bacteria. Treatment is more complicated,
often requiring surgical intervention, alignment, and sterilization to prevent complications. While both are
fractures, compound fractures are more dangerous due to their severity and potential complications.
