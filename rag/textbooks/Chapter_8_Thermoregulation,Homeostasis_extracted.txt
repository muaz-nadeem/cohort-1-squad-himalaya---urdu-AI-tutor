Living organisms must maintain a stable internal environment to survive, even when external conditions
change. This ability is called homeostasis, and one of its most important aspects is the regulation of body
temperature, known as thermoregulation.

Temperature strongly influences the speed of biochemical reactions, the structure of proteins, and the
functioning of enzymes. If body temperature rises too high (hyperthermia), enzymes may denature and vital
processes can collapse. If temperature falls too low (hypothermia), reaction rates slow down and cells cannot
function properly. Thus, keeping body temperature within an optimal range is essential for life.

Different animals have evolved various strategies to cope with temperature fluctuations. Some, like fisi.es,
reptiles, and amphibians, rely largely on external heat sources and are.called ectotherms. Others, such as birds
and mammals, generate and regulate their own body heat, and are termed endotherms.

In humans, thermoregulation is controlled by the hypothalamus, which acts as a thermostat. It receives
information from temperature receptors, compares it with the set point (around 37 °C), and activates responses
through effectors such as sweat: glands, blood vessels, and muscles. These responses may involve heat-loss
mechanisms (like sweating and vasodilation) or heat-gain mechanisms (like shivering and vasoconstriction).

Understanding thermoregulation not only explains how organisms maintain balance with their environment
but also highlights the intricate coordination of receptors, control centers, and effectors that sustain life.

THERMOREGULATION

Thermoregulation is the biological process by which organisms maintain a stable internal body temperature
within a narrow and optimal range, despite fluctuations in the external environment. It involves physiological,
behavioral, and structural adaptations that prevent harmful variations in body temperature. In humans and other
warm-blooded animals, thermoregulation is primarily controlled by the hypothalamus, which acts as the body’s

“thermostat.”

Enzyme Function and Metabolism

Enzymes are highly specialized proteins that act as catalysts for almost every biochemical reaction occurring
in living cells. Their activity is strongly dependent on temperature, as they function efficiently only within a
narrow optimal range. In humans, this optimal temperature is around 37 °C. If body temperature rises
beyond this range, a condition called hyperthermia occurs. Under hyperthermia, enzymes begin to lose their
three-dimensional shape, a process known as denaturation, which severely reduces their ability to catalyze
reactions. As.a result, essential processes such as respiration, digestion, and protein synthesis slow down or
may even cease entirely. On the other hand, if the body’s temperature falls below the normal range,
hypothermia sets in. At low temperatures, enzymes do not denature but their activity becomes sluggish,
leading to a sharp decline in metabolic rate. This reduced metabolic efficiency compromises the production
of energy and weakens cellular functions. Thus, thermoregulation is vital for maintaining the precise
temperature conditions necessary for enzyme activity and overall metabolism.

Cell and Organ Protection

Temperature extremes pose a direct threat to the integrity of cellular structures and organs. At high
temperatures, proteins and lipids that form the building blocks of cells undergo structural damage. Proteins
may unfold or clump together, disrupting normal cellular machinery, while lipids in membranes become
excessively fluid, weakening the barrier that protects cells. Nucleic acids such as DNA and RNA are also
sensitive to high heat, which can cause mutations or breakage of their molecular bonds. Conversely, at very
low temperatures, cells face the risk of ice crystal formation within their cytoplasm. These ice crystals are
sharp and can puncture delicate membranes and organelles, leading to irreversible damage and cell death.
Vital organs such as the heart, liver, and kidneys are especially at risk in such conditions, since their failure
can quickly threaten survival. Thermoregulation, by keeping body temperature within safe limits, provides
essential protection against these potentially lethal effects.

Homeostasis of Vital Processes

The maintenance of a constant body temperature is fundamental to the smooth functioning of vital
physiological processes. For example, respiration depends on temperature-sensitive enzymes in
mitochondria to generate ATP efficiently. Circulation is also influenced, as both high and low temperatures
alter the viscosity of blood and the efficiency of the heart in pumping it. Nerve conduction, which relies on
precise ion movements across membranes, becomes slowed or erratic when temperatures deviate from the
normal range. Organs such as the brain and the heart are especially sensitive to temperature disturbances.
The brain requires a steady environment to maintain consciousness, coordination, and memory, while the
heart depends on precise biochemical signals for rhythmic contractions. Even a slight increase above normal
body temperature can lead to confusion, seizures, or cardiac stress, while a drop below the normal range
can result in loss of coordination or unconsciousness. Therefore, thermoregulation is indispensable in
sustaining the internal environment required for these vital processes.

Survival in Fluctuating Environments

Living organisms are constantly exposed to environmental changes, such as day-to-night temperature
variations, seasonal shifts between summer and winter, and geographic differences between hot deserts
and cold polar regions. Without thermoregulation, organisms would not be able to adapt to these
fluctuations and would risk death during unfavorable conditions. For instance, desert animals must prevent
overheating during the day and avoid chilling during the night, while polar animals need insulation and
behavioral mechanisms to withstand freezing conditions. Thermoregulation enables animals to remain
active, find food, reproduce, and escape predators in both hot and cold habitats. In humans, it allows us to
live in and adapt to diverse environments across the globe—from the scorching tropics to icy mountain
ranges—by maintaining internal temperature stability despite extreme external variations. Thus,
thermoregulation ensures survival and functionality in ever-changing climates.

Energy Balance

Thermoregulation also plays a crucial role in managing the body’s energy resources. Maintaining body
temperature within a stable range minimizes unnecessary energy expenditure, allowing organisms to direct
energy toward growth, reproduction, and activity rather than continuous adjustment to environmental

CLASSIFICATION OF ANIMALS ON THE BASIS OF THERMOREGULATION

Animals differ in how they regulate their internal body temperature when exposed to environmental
changes. Based on this ability, they are classified into two broad categories: poikilotherms and homeotherms.
This classification highlights the distinct strategies animals have evolved to cope with the thermal challenges of
their habitats.

1. Poikilotherms (Cold-blooded Animals)

Poikilotherms are animals whose internal body temperature fluctuates with the temperature of their
external environment. They do not possess efficient physiological mechanisms to maintain a constant
internal temperature. Instead, their metabolic activities and body functions rise or fall according to ambient
conditions. For example, when exposed to heat, poikilotherms become more active due to an increase in
enzymatic activity, whereas in colder environments, their activity slows down drastically.

The lack of internal regulation makes poikilotherms heavily dependent on environmental conditions for
survival. To cope, they rely on behavioral adaptations, such as basking in the sun to raise body temperature
or seeking shade, burrows, or water to avoid overheating. Many reptiles, amphibians, fishes, and
invertebrates are poikilothermic. For instance, lizards are often seen basking on rocks in the morning to
absorb heat, and frogs become sluggish during winter as their body processes slow down.

While poikilothermy conserves energy—because these animals do not burn large amounts of food for heat
production—it also restricts them. They cannot remain active in extreme cold or heat for long periods, and
their distribution is limited to environments where temperature fluctuations are within tolerable limits.

Homeotherms (Warm-blooded Animals)

Homeotherms are animals that maintain a relatively constant internal body temperature, regardless of
external environmental conditions. This is achieved through well-developed physiological mechanisms,
such as sweating, panting, shivering, vasodilation, vasoconstriction, and changes in metabolic rate. The

hypothalamus acts as the central control system, ensuring that the body’s temperature remains within a

narrow range (for humans, typically around 36.5—37.5 °C).

Because homeotherms regulate their own temperature, they can remain active across a wide range of

environmental conditions, from deserts to polar regions. Birds and mammals are classic examples of

homeotherms. For instance, polar bears maintain body warmth through thick fur and fat insulation even in
freezing conditions, while camels regulate heat storage and water balance to survive in hot deserts.

However, maintaining homeothermy requires a high metabolic rate, which in turn demands a continuous

supply of food and oxygen. This makes homeotherms energetically more expensive than poikilotherms.

Despite this cost, the advantage lies in their ability to survive, reproduce, and remain active in diverse and

often harsh climates.

In conclusion, animals can be classified into two groups based on thermoregulation: poikilotherms, which
allow their body temperature to fluctuate with the environment, and homeotherms, which maintain a stable
internal temperature through physiological regulation. Poikilothermy conserves energy but restricts activity in
extreme climates, while homeothermy allows independence from environmental conditions but requires greater
energy expenditure. Both strategies illustrate the diversity of adaptations that animals have evolved to survive in
their respective habitats.

CLASSIFICATION OF ANIMALS ON THE BASIS OF THE SOURCE OF BODY HEAT

Animals.can also be classified according to the primary. source from which they obtain body heat. This
classification distinguishes between organisms that rely on the external environment for heat and those that
generate it internally through metabolic processes. On this basis, animals are grouped into two main categories:
ectotherms and endotherms.

1. Ectotherms

Ectotherms are animals that depend primarily on heat gained from their external environment to regulate
body temperature. Their internal heat production through metabolism is relatively low and insufficient to
maintain a stable temperature on its own. As a result, ectothermic animals exhibit wide fluctuations in body
temperature that Closely mirror environmental changes.

To survive, ectotherms adopt behavioral strategies to regulate heat. For example, reptiles like snakes and
lizards bask in the sun to absorb heat and raise their body temperature for activity, while in excessively hot
conditions they retreat to burrows, shaded areas, or water bodies to avoid overheating. Amphibians, fishes,
and most invertebrates are also ectothermic, relying on the warmth of the sun or surrounding water to
function efficiently.

The advantage of ectothermy is that it conserves energy, as these animals do not burn large amounts of
food to generate heat internally. This allows them to survive on smaller food supplies compared to
endotherms. However, the disadvantage is their dependence on environmental temperatures. In cold
conditions, ectotherms become sluggish and unable to maintain high levels-of activity, which limits their
survival in extreme or fluctuating climates.

2. Endotherms

Endotherms are animals that generate most of their body heat through internal metabolic processes, such

as cellular respiration. This internally produced heat allows them to maintain a relatively constant body

temperature, independent of the external environment. Endotherms use both physiological and structural
adaptations to balance heat production and heat loss. Examples include shivering to produce heat, sweating
or panting to cool down, insulating fur or feathers, and fat layers such as blubber in whales and seals.

Birds and mammals are typical endotherms. A sparrow, for example, maintains high body temperature to

sustain flight even in cool mornings, while polar bears and arctic foxes use thick fur and fat insulation to

survive freezing polar regions. Endothermy provides a significant advantage by enabling activity in a wide
range of habitats and climates, as well as during both day and night.

The cost of endothermy, however, is high energy demand. Since internal heat production requires rapid

metabolism, endotherms must consume large amounts of food and oxygen. This constant energy

requirement makes them more vulnerable to food scarcity, but it is balanced by the benefit of environmental
independence and continuous activity.

Animals are classified on the basis of their heat source into ectotherms, which rely on environmental heat
and show fluctuating body temperatures, and endotherms, which generate their own heat metabolically and
maintain stable body temperatures. Ectotherms conserve energy but are dependent on external conditions, while
endotherms remain independent of the environment but require large amounts of energy. This classification
highlights the contrasting strategies animals use to meet the universal challenge of temperature regulation.

REGULATORY STRATEGIES IN MAN FOR THERMOREGULATION

Humans, being homeothermic and endothermic animals, maintain their internal body temperature within a
narrow range of about 36.5—37.5 °C, regardless of environmental fluctuations. This regulation is controlled mainly

by the hypothalamus, which functions as the body’s thermostat. The hypothalamus constantly monitors blood
temperature and, when deviations are detected, initiates physiological and behavioral responses to restore
balance. These regulatory strategies can be divided into two categories: responses to overheating (heat-loss
mechanisms) and responses to excessive cooling (heat-gain mechanisms).

Heat-Loss Mechanisms (Regulation in Hot Conditions)

When the external temperature is high, or when internal heat is generated through strenuous muscular
activity, the human body must prevent overheating to maintain homeostasis. Overheating can be
dangerous, as it disrupts enzymatic functions, accelerates dehydration, and may lead to heat stroke. To avoid
these outcomes, the hypothalamus activates several coordinated mechanisms that promote heat loss and
restore body temperature to normal.

1.

Sweating (Evaporative Cooling): Sweating is the most effective cooling mechanism in humans. Sweat
glands located in the dermis release fluid onto the surface of the skin. As this sweat evaporates, it
absorbs a large amount of latent heat from the body, thereby cooling the blood flowing beneath the
skin. This process works best in hot and dry environments where evaporation is rapid. However, in
humid conditions, evaporation is slowed down, which makes sweating less efficient and explains the
discomfort experienced in such climates.

Vasodilation of Skin Arterioles: Another key response is vasodilation, in which blood vessels near the
skin surface widen. This increases the flow of warm blood close to the surface, where heat can escape
into the environment through radiation, convection, and conduction. The skin often becomes flushed
or reddish as a result of increased blood flow. By channeling heat outward, vasodilation prevents
dangerous rises in core body temperature.

Reduced Metabolic Rate: The body also regulates its internal heat production by lowering the metabolic
rate. By slowing down chemical reactions and energy release, the production of extra heat within cells
is minimized. This conservation of energy reduces the risk of further overheating and supports the body
in maintaining balance.

Behavioral Adjustments: In addition to physiological mechanisms, humans consciously adopt behavioral
strategies to regulate temperature. Examples include wearing light and loose-fitting clothing, drinking
water to replace fluids lost through sweating, reducing strenuous activity, and seeking cooler
environments such as shade or air-conditioned spaces. These behaviors complement internal regulatory
mechanisms and play a crucial role in preventing dehydration and heat-related illnesses.

Through the combined actions of sweating, vasodilation, reduced metabolism, and behavioral strategies,
the body maintains a safe internal temperature during hot conditions. This integrated system ensures the
protection of enzymes, cells, and vital organs from the harmful effects of hyperthermia, thereby preserving
normal physiological function even under extreme heat. 7

Heat-Gain Mechanisms (Regulation in Cold Conditions)

In cold environments, the body must conserve existing heat and produce additional warmth to prevent
hypothermia. A fall in body temperature slows down enzyme activity, reduces metabolic efficiency, and
threatens vital organ function. To counter these effects, the hypothalamus activates a series of physiological
and behavioral mechanisms designed to retain or generate heat.

Shivering: One of the most immediate responses to cold is shivering, which consists of rapid, involuntary
contractions of skeletal muscles. These contractions require energy and therefore increase the rate of
cellular respiration, which in turn produces heat as a by-product. Although shivering can be physically
tiring, it is highly effective in raising body temperature during sudden exposure to cold.
Vasoconstriction of Skin Arterioles: Another important strategy is vasoconstriction, in which arterioles
supplying blood to the skin narrow. This reduces the flow of warm blood to the surface, thereby
minimizing heat loss through radiation, convection, and conduction. As a result, the skin may appear
pale or bluish, particularly in the fingers, toes, and lips. By preserving heat in the body’s core,
vasoconstriction helps protect vital organs from dangerously low temperatures.

Piloerection (Goosebumps): The contraction of tiny muscles at the base of hair follicles causes hairs to
stand upright, a response known as piloerection or “goosebumps.” In ancestral mammals with dense
fur, this mechanism trapped an insulating layer of air close to the skin, reducing heat loss. In humans,
where body hair is sparse, this response is largely ineffective for insulation but remains as a vestigial
mechanism. ;

Increased Metabolic Rate: Cold exposure also stimulates hormonal responses that increase heat
production. The thyroid gland secretes thyroxine, which raises the basal metabolic rate, while the
adrenal medulla releases adrenaline, which accelerates metabolism and enhances heat generation.
These hormonal adjustments ensure a sustained supply of energy and warmth, especially during
prolonged exposure to cold conditions.

Behavioral Adjustments: in addition to automatic physiological responses, humans consciously adopt
behaviors that help conserve and generate heat. These include wearing insulating clothing, covering
exposed body parts, seeking shelter from wind and cold, consuming warm food and drinks, and engaging
in physical activities such as exercise to stimulate muscle activity and heat production.

Through the combined action of shivering, vasoconstriction, piloerection, metabolic adjustments, and
behavioral strategies, the human body maintains core temperature within the normal range. These
mechanisms are critical for keeping the brain, heart, and other essential organs functional, ensuring survival
in cold environments and preventing the life-threatening effects of hypothermia.

HOMEOSTASIS AND ITS ROLE IN THERMOREGULATION

Homeostasis is the process by which living organisms maintain a stable internal environment despite
constant changes in the external surroundings. It ensures that essential conditions such as temperature, pH, water
balance, and glucose concentration remain within narrow limits necessary for survival. In humans, homeostasis is
critical because enzymes and cellular processes can function properly only under stable internal conditions.

One of the most important examples of homeostasis is thermoregulation—the regulation of body
temperature. For humans, the normal internal body temperature is around 36.5-37.5 °C. If this range is disturbed,
enzymes may lose efficiency, cells may suffer damage, and vital organs such as the brain and heart may fail to
function correctly. To prevent such dangers, the body uses a coordinated system of receptors, a control center,
and effectors to detect changes, process information, and restore balance.

Elements of Homeostatic Mechanisms

Homeostasis in humans and other animals is achieved through a highly coordinated system that involves
three essential components: receptors (sensors), a control center (integrator), and effectors (responders). These
three elements form a feedback loop that continuously monitors the internal environment, detects changes, and
restores balance whenever necessary. Together, they ensure that vital conditions such as temperature, pH, water
balance, and glucose concentration remain within safe limits.
1. Receptors (Sensors)

Receptors are specialized cells or structures that detect changes, known as stimuli, in the body’s internal or

external environment. Their primary role is to monitor conditions and immediately send information to the

control center.

In thermoregulation, two major types of receptors are involved:

e Peripheral Thermoreceptors: Located in the skin, these receptors sense changes in the external
environment. For example, when the skin comes in contact with cold air or hot surfaces, the peripheral
thermoreceptors detect these variations and send signals to the brain. This allows the body to prepare

e Central Thermoreceptors: Found mainly in the hypothalamus, these receptors monitor the temperature
of the blood as it circulates through the brain. Since blood temperature reflects the body’s core
temperature, central receptors provide the most accurate information about internal conditions.

Both sets of receptors work together: peripheral receptors act as an early-warning system for external

changes, while central receptors provide precise monitoring of internal stability. This continuous flow of

information ensures rapid detection of any deviation from the set point, allowing corrective measures to be
initiated without delay.
2. Control Center (Integrator)

The control center is the decision-making component of the homeostatic system, It receives information
from receptors, compares it with the body’s normal set point, and initiates responses to correct any imbalance.

HOMEOSTATIC MECHANISMS AND FEEDBACK SYSTEMS

Homeostasis operates through feedback systems, which are self-regulating processes that maintain internal
stability in response to external or internal changes. Feedback can be of two types: negative feedback and positive
feedback. Both play vital roles in regulating physiological processes, but they differ in how they influence the
original stimulus.

1. Negative Feedback Systems

Negative feedback is the most common and vital type of feedback in homeostatic regulation. In this system,

the response generated by effectors opposes or reverses the initial change, thereby restoring balance to

the normal set point.

> — Role in Homeostasis
Most homeostatic mechanisms in the human body, including thermoregulation, blood sugar control, and
water balance, are governed by negative feedback. It prevents extreme deviations and maintains conditions
within narrow limits essential for enzyme activity and cellular function.

2. Positive Feedback Systems

Positive feedback is less common in homeostasis. In this system, the response generated by- effectors

amplifies or intensifies the original stimulus, pushing the variable further away from the set point. This loop

continues until an external factor interrupts it.
> Role in the Body

Positive feedback is usually associated with processes that need a rapid, decisive outcome rather than

ongoing balance. While it is not the main mechanism of homeostasis, it can temporarily override normal

stability for specific biological purposes.

Examples:

Blood Clotting:.When a blood vessel is injured, platelets adhere to the damaged site and release
chemical signals that attract additional platelets. This process accelerates until a sufficient clot is formed
to seal the wound. Once the injury is resolved, the feedback loop terminates.

signals for increased oxytocin secretion. This amplifies contractions until childbirth occurs, at which point
the stimulus (cervical stretch) ceases and the loop ends.

Define osmoregulation.

OSMOREGULATION

Osmoregulation is the biological process through which living organisms regulate the balance of water and
dissolved solutes—such as salts, ions, and other osmolytes—in their body fluids. It ensures that the osmotic
pressure inside cells and tissues remains stable, preventing harmful effects of excessive water gain (swelling) or
water loss (shrinkage).

Importance

Maintaining osmotic baiance is essential because all metabolic activities occur in an aqueous medium. If
water and solute concentrations fluctuate widely, enzyme function, cellular transport, and overall metabolism are
disrupted. Osmoregulation therefore plays a vital role in maintaining homeostasis, ensuring that cells function
efficiently regardless of external environmental changes.
How It Works?

Osmoregulation involves three main components:

1. Receptors (Osmoreceptors): Specialized sensors, mainly in the hypothalamus, detect changes in water
concentration and osmotic pressure of blood plasma.

2. Control Center: The hypothalamus processes this information and, if needed, signals the pituitary gland
to release antidiuretic hormone (ADH).

3. Effectors: The kidneys act as the primary effectors. They adjust the volume and concentration of urine
by either conserving water when the body is dehydrated or excreting excess water when the body is
overhydrated.

Examples:

© Inhumans, when water levels drop (e.g., due to sweating), ADH secretion increases. This makes kidney
tubules reabsorb more water, producing concentrated urine.

© When water intake is high, ADH secretion decreases, leading to dilute urine and restoring osmotic balance.

PROBLEMS FACED BY OSMOREGULATORS

Osmoregulators actively maintain a constant internal balance of water and solutes, regardless of external
environmental conditions. While this ability allows them to survive in diverse habitats, it also presents several
challenges. These problems differ depending on whether the animal lives in freshwater, marine, or terrestrial
environments.

1. Freshwater Osmoregulators

Freshwater animals live in an environment where the concentration of salts outside is much lower than

inside their body fluids. This creates a constant osmotic challenge.

Problems Faced: d

© Water Influx by Osmosis: Water continuously enters the body across permeable surfaces (skin or gills).

This can cause cells to swell and burst if not regulated.

© Loss of Salts: Essential ions like sodium and chloride tend to diffuse out of the body into the dilute

environment.

Consequences: Without regulation, freshwater animals would become diluted and lose critical electrolytes.
2. Marine Osmoregulators

Marine animals live in seawater, which has a much higher salt concentration than their body fluids. This

creates the opposite osmotic stress compared to freshwater animals.

Problems Faced:

¢ Loss of Water by Osmosis: Water tends to leave the body, leading to dehydration.

e Excess Salt Gain: Salt enters the body across gill membranes and from drinking seawater.

Consequences: Without regulation, marine osmoregulators would become dehydrated and accumulate

toxic levels of salts.

3. Terrestrial Osmoregulators
Land animals face challenges due to exposure to air and a relatively dry environment.
Problems Faced:
Water Loss by Evaporation: Evaporation from skin, respiratory surfaces, and excretion can rapidly
deplete body water.
© Limited Water Availability: Many terrestrial habitats have scarce or irregular sources of fresh water.
e Salt Balance: Sweating and excretion can lead to loss of essential electrolytes.
Consequences: Without mechanisms to conserve water, terrestrial animals risk dehydration and impaired
physiological functions.
4. Energy Demands of Osmoregulation

Acommon problem across all environments is the high energy cost of osmoregulation. Active transport of
ions across membranes, production of concentrated or dilute urine, and specialized glandular secretions
(e.g., salt glands in marine birds) all consume large amounts of ATP. This means osmoregulators must
balance energy expenditure for survival with other biological needs like growth and reproduction.
Osmoregulators face significant problems depending on their habitat:

e Freshwater animals struggle with excess water gain and salt loss.

© Marine animals struggle with dehydration and salt overload.

& Terrestrial animals face water loss through evaporation and limited water supply.

Osmoregulation in Freshwater Animals

Freshwater animals live in a hypotonic environment, where the concentration of solutes in the surrounding

water is lower than that of their body fluids. Consequently, water continuously enters their bodies by

osmosis, and they face a risk of salt loss.

Freshwater is a hypotonic environment, meaning the concentration of solutes outside the body is lower

than inside body fluids. As a result, water continuously enters the body by osmosis, while essential salts tend

to diffuse out. Without regulation, cells would swell and burst, and electrolytes would be depleted.

Adaptations of Freshwater Animals:

e Excretion of Dilute Urine: Freshwater fishes and amphibians produce large volumes of dilute urine,
which removes excess water from the body.

Salt Reabsorption by Kidneys: The kidneys actively reabsorb useful ions such as sodium and chloride to
minimize their loss.

¢ Active Salt Uptake: Specialized cells called ionocytes (chloride cells), located in the gills of fishes and
the skin of amphibians, actively transport salts from the water into the body.

© Dietary Compensation: Salts lost to the environment are replaced through food intake.

Example: Most freshwater fishes (carps, tilapia) and amphibians (frogs) are osmoregulators, maintaining

body fluids at higher solute concentrations than their environment.

Osmoregulation in Marine Animals

Marine teleosts (bony fishes) live in a hypertonic environment, where the concentration of solutes in

seawater exceeds that of their internal body fluids. As a result, they tend to lose water to the environment

by osmosis, particularly across the gill epithelium, and face an accumulation of excess salts due to ingestion

of seawater.

Adaptations of Marine Teleosts (Bony Fishes):

e Drinking Seawater: Marine fishes drink seawater to replace water lost by osmosis.

¢ Active Salt Excretion:
© Monovalent ions (Na*, CL”, K*) are excreted through chloride cells in the gills.
o Divalent ions (Mg**, Ca**, SO,?") are excreted via the kidneys.

© Specialized Salt Glands: Some species possess rectal glands (e.g., in sharks) that eliminate excess salts
into the digestive tract.

¢ Partial Osmoconformation in Elasmobranchs: Sharks and rays retain urea and trimethylamine oxide
(TMAO) in their blood, making body fluids nearly isotonic with seawater and reducing osmotic stress.

Examples:

© Marine bony fishes like tuna and cod rely heavily on gill and kidney functions.

© Sharks use urea retention along with rectal glands.

Marine birds (seagulls) and reptiles (sea turtles) use salt glands to expel concentrated saline solutions.

surroundings, so preventing water loss is a major challenge.
