BIOLOGICAL WARFARE

Biological warfare (BW), also known as bioterrorism or germ warfare, is the deliberate use of pathogenic
microorganisms or their toxins to cause disease, death, or fear in humans, animals, or plants. Unlike conventional
weapons, biological agents can multiply and spread, making them particularly dangerous and unpredictable.
Historical Overview of Biological Warfare

The use of biological warfare dates back to ancient times when early civilizations employed biological
methods to incapacitate or eliminate their enemies. One of the earliest recorded instances occurred in 590 BC,
when the Assyrians, an ancient civilization of Mesopotamia (modern-day Iraq), reportedly contaminated enemy
water supplies with bread infected with Ergot—a parasitic fungus that produces toxins causing hallucinations and
paralysis.

A significant historical example occurred in 1347, when the Mongol army besieging the port city of Caffa (in
present-day Crimea, not Ethiopia) catapulted plague-infected corpses over the city walls. This act is believed to
have contributed to the spread of the Black Death into Europe, leading to the death of millions and triggering
massive social and economic disruption.

During the 16th century, as European powers colonized the Americas, Spanish and other European
colonizers used smallpox as a biological weapon against Indigenous populations. The disease devastated native
communities, reducing their capacity to resist foreign domination. Another documented case of biological warfare
occurred in 1763, during the French and Indian War, when British forces deliberately distributed blankets
contaminated with smallpox to Native American tribes. This resulted in deadly outbreaks and widespread
suffering among Indigenous populations.

In the 20th century, scientific and technological advancements enabled more sophisticated applications of
biological warfare. During World War |, Germany attempted to infect horses and livestock used by Allied forces
with pathogens like Bacillus anthracis (anthrax) and Burkholderia mallei (glanders). Though the impact was
limited, these efforts marked the beginning of modern biological warfare.

In World War Il, Japan’s Unit 731 conducted large-scale biological warfare experiments in China, deploying
pathogens such as the plague bacterium (Yersinia pestis), Vibrio cholerae (cholera), and Bacillus anthracis
(anthrax). These inhumane experiments led to the deaths of thousands and demonstrated the catastrophic
potential of biological weapons.

During the Cold War, both the United States and the Soviet Union developed extensive biological weapons
programs, stockpiling pathogens like smallpox and Francisella tularensis (tularemia). A tragic incident occurred in
the Soviet city of Sverdlovsk in 1979, where an accidental release of anthrax spores from a military facility resulted
in numerous civilian deaths—highlighting the dangers of biological weapons research and accidental exposure.

In 2001, a modern instance of biological warfare occurred when letters containing anthrax spores were
mailed to media outlets and U.S. government offices. These bioterrorist attacks resulted in five deaths, multiple
infections, and widespread fear, triggering major responses from public health and security agencies.

Throughout history, human societies have engaged in conflicts and wars using various strategies to gain
military or political advantage over adversaries. Traditional warfare involved organized armies and direct combat,
while guerrilla warfare relied on irregular tactics such as ambushes and sabotage. In the modern era, the landscape
of conflict has expanded to include cyber warfare and biological warfare, both of which pose complex and far-
reaching threats.

Current State of Biological Warfare

Today, biological warfare remains'a pressing concern due to rapid advances in molecular biology, genetics,
and biotechnology. Modern techniques allow for the genetic engineering of pathogens to enhance their
virulence, resistance to existing treatments, and ease of transmission—making them potentially more dangerous
as weapons.

A major turning point in the perception of biological threats occurred in 2002, when scientists successfully
synthesized the polio virus in a laboratory. This raised significant alarms regarding the creation of synthetic
pathogens that could be weaponized. Another controversial example involves gain-of-function research on the
H5N1 avian influenza virus, where modifications increased its transmissibility among mammals. These
developments have intensified debates about dual-use research—scientific work that can be used for both
beneficial and harmful purposes.

Although many countries are signatories to the Biological Weapons Convention (BWC)—an international
treaty banning the development, production, and use of biological weapons—the potential for misuse persists.
The ease of access to biotechnological tools and the dual-use nature of biological research continue to pose
serious challenges for global biosecurity.

Governments worldwide are investing in biodefense programs, early-warning systems, disease surveillance,
and public health preparedness to respond to potential biological attacks. However, the effectiveness of such
measures depends on international cooperation, transparency in research, and strict regulatory frameworks.

High Lethality or Morbidity

An effective BW agent often causes severe disease or death. The lethality refers to the ability to cause death

in a significant proportion of exposed individuals, while morbidity refers to the ability to cause serious

illness, even if not fatal. Example: Bacillus anthracis (anthrax) spores can cause fatal respiratory infections,
and Clostridium botulinum produces botulinum toxin, which can paralyze and kill in minute amounts. High
lethality ensures that the agent achieves its intended disruptive or terror-inducing effect.

Ability to Spread Easily Among Populations

BW agents must be transmissible to maximize impact. This can occur through airborne particles,

contaminated food or water, or direct contact. Example: Yersinia pestis, the bacterium causing plague, can

be spread via respiratory droplets, while smallpox spreads from person to person through direct contact.

Agents with high transmissibility can lead to epidemics or pandemics, overwhelming public health systems

and creating widespread fear.

Stability in the Environment for Transport or Storage

Effective BW agents must survive outside a host long enough to be transported, stored, and deployed.

Environmental stability includes resistance to heat, desiccation, UV radiation, and chemical disinfectants.

Example: Anthrax spores remain viable in soil for decades, making them suitable for long-term storage and

deployment. Environmental stability ensures that even small quantities of an agent can be effective after

storage or shipment, increasing its potential for strategic use.

Difficulty in Diagnosis or Treatment

BW agents often cause symptoms that are nonspecific or resemble common illnesses, delaying diagnosis.

Slow recognition allows the infection to spread and increases the challenge of treatment. Example: Early

symptoms of inhalation anthrax or viral hemorrhagic fevers resemble flu-like illnesses, making initial

detection difficult. Limited availability of vaccines, specific antibiotics, or antiviral therapies adds to the
disruption caused by the agent. Psychological impact: The uncertainty and fear generated by delayed
diagnosis or ineffective treatment can amplify social and economic disruption.

Additional Considerations (Enrichment)

e Ease of Production: Agents that can be cultured or synthesized in a laboratory are more accessible for
misuse.

e Low Infectious Dose: Pathogens that cause disease even at very low exposure levels are particularly
dangerous. Example: Botulinum toxin can cause fatal disease with tiny amounts.

¢ Potential for Aerosolization: Agents that can be dispersed as fine particles can infect large populations
quickly.

Impacts of Biological Warfare

The consequences of biological warfare are wide-ranging and devastating. Deliberate release of infectious

agents can lead to massive public health crises, causing high mortality rates, overwhelming healthcare

systems, and creating long-term medical challenges for affected populations.

e Economically, biological warfare can severely disrupt trade, damage agricultural industries, and lead to
significant losses in productivity and food security. Diseases targeting livestock and crops can devastate
local economies, especially in regions heavily reliant on agriculture.

¢ Social impacts include widespread panic, fear, and breakdowns in community cohesion and trust. The
psychological trauma associated with biological attacks can be long-lasting, fostering social unrest and
deepening inequalities within affected communities.

© Politically, the use or even threat of biological weapons can strain international relations, provoke
military responses, and lead to heightened global tensions. Governments may implement extreme
security measures, sometimes infringing on civil liberties, to contain the threat.

Given these multidimensional impacts, there is an urgent global need for coordinated action in biosecurity,

epidemic preparedness, and ethical oversight of biological research. Strengthening global health systems,

enforcing international treaties, and promoting transparency in scientific research are essential steps toward
reducing the risk of biological warfare and ensuring the safety of future generations.

BIODEFENSE

Biodefense refers to a comprehensive set of strategies, technologies, and actions implemented to protect
populations from the threat of biological warfare and bioterrorism. It encompasses both proactive and reactive
measures aimed at preventing, detecting, and responding to biological threats—whether these arise from natural
outbreaks or intentional release of pathogenic agents.

The foundational principles of biodefense provide a strategic framework to ensure preparedness,
prevention, rapid response, containment, and recovery. These principles are essential for safeguarding pu!

Health, national security, and global stability.

extent is it valid or overblown?

omIcS

In recent years, the emergence of the 'omics' sciences—including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics,
and metabolomics—has significantly transformed the landscape of biological and biomedical research. These
interconnected disciplines offer comprehensive insights into the structure, function, and dynamic regulation of
biological systems, enabling more precise and systems-level understanding of life processes.

The promises of ‘omics' technologies are far-reaching, from disease diagnosis and treatment to agricultural
improvements and personalized health interventions. However, despite the remarkable progress and potential
applications, it is crucial to critically evaluate the actual capabilities and limitations of these technologies. While
optimism is warranted, it must be tempered by a realistic assessment of current challenges, technical constraints,
and ethical concerns. As research continues to advance, the true potential of 'omics' can be realized in ways that
profoundly benefit both science and society.

Genomics

Genomics is the study of the complete set of DNA—referred to as the genome—within an organism. This
field includes the identification, sequencing, mapping, and analysis of all genes and non-coding regions, aiming to
understand how genetic variation influences biological function, development, and disease.

A landmark achievement in genomics was the Human Genome Project, completed in 2003, which
successfully sequenced the entire human genome and identified all human genes. This project laid the foundation
for personalized medicine, enabling treatment plans tailored to an individual’s genetic profile.

Applications of genomics include:

e Genetic testing for identifying predisposition to certain diseases,

e Ancestry analysis, and

Pharmacogenomics, which studies how genes influence drug response.

Despite these advancements, the potential of genomics is sometimes overstated. For example, the belief
that all genetic disorders can be cured solely through genomic information remains largely aspirational. Moreover,
ethical concerns such as genetic privacy, potential discrimination, and the ownership of genetic data raise serious
societal questions. Genomics, while powerful, is one piece of a complex biological puzzle and must be integrated
with other ‘omics’ data for holistic understanding.

Transcriptomics

Transcriptomics is the study of the-transcriptome, which encompasses all RNA molecules—including
messenger RNA (mRNA), non-coding RNA, and other RNA types—expressed in a cell or tissue at a given time. This
field provides insights into gene expression patterns and regulatory mechanisms by examining how genes are
transcribed in different conditions or disease states.

One key application is in cancer research, where transcriptomic profiling -helps identify genes that are
overexpressed or silenced in tumor cells, aiding in the discovery of novel therapeutic targets. In agriculture,
transcriptomics is used to investigate how crops respond to environmental stresses such as drought, salinity, or
pathogen attack. By understanding gene expression under stress, researchers can develop more resilient crop
varieties.

While transcriptomics has greatly enhanced our understanding of gene regulation, it is important to note
that RNA expression levels do not always correlate directly with protein abundance or function. Additionally, the
interpretation of transcriptomic data requires validation and integration with proteomic and metabolomic
information to form a complete picture of cellular behavior.

Proteomics

Proteomics is the large-scale study of proteins—the key functional molecules within cells. Proteomics
investigates protein expression, structure, interactions, modifications, and functions, offering valuable insight
into cellular processes, disease mechanisms, and therapeutic development.

Proteomic research has led to the identification of biomarkers for diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease,
cardiovascular disorders, and various cancers, enabling early diagnosis and monitoring of disease progression.
Moreover, proteomics contributes to drug discovery by revealing specific proteins that can be targeted by
pharmaceutical agents.

However, the proteome is highly complex and dynamic. Proteins undergo post-translational modifications,
interact in intricate networks, and vary across different cell types and conditions. These features pose significant
analytical challenges. In addition, the high costs and technical demands of proteomic tools—such as mass
spectrometry—currently limit their routine use in clinical practice.

Despite these challenges, proteomics .remains.a cornerstone of systems biology and continues to drive
innovation in biomedical research.

Metabolomics

Metabolomics involves the comprehensive analysis of metabolites—small molecules that are intermediates
or end-products of cellular metabolism. Because metabolites reflect the biochemical activity within a cell, tissue,
or organism, metabolomics provides a snapshot of the physiological state under specific conditions.

Metabolomic studies are used in numerous fields:

e In human health, metabolomics can uncover how different diets influence metabolism and help create

personalized nutrition plans.

e In medical diagnostics, distinct metabolite patterns (metabolomic profiles) can indicate metabolic

disorders, cancer, or infectious diseases, facilitating earlier and more accurate diagnoses.

e In environmental science, it helps assess the impact of pollutants on organisms and ecosystems.

Despite its potential, metabolomics faces key limitations. The chemical diversity of metabolites, variations
in concentration, and the influence of external factors—such as diet, microbiome, and environment—make
consistent data collection and interpretation challenging. The field requires further technological refinement and
data integration with other 'omics' to enhance its accuracy and reliability.

SYNTHETIC BIOLOGY

Synthetic biology is an interdisciplinary and rapidly evolving field that merges biology with engineering
principles, computer science, and biotechnology to design, construct, and modify biological parts, systems, and
organisms. Its aim is to either create.entirely new biological functions or redesign existing biological components
for useful applications. By combining knowledge from molecular biology, genetic engineering, computational

--- Page 10 ---

modelling, and systems biology, synthetic biology holds the potential to revolutionize fields such as medicine,
agriculture, industrial biotechnology, and environmental management.

At its core, synthetic biology operates on key principles that guide the rational design and predictable
behaviour of biological systems:

Standardization: Biological parts are standardized into reusable components, often called BioBricks,
which can be assembled in various combinations to produce desired biological functions.

Modularity: Systems are built from interchangeable modules that function independently and can be
easily integrated or replaced.

Orthogonality: Designed components are made to function without interfering with the host’s native
biological processes.

Abstraction: Complex systems are broken down into hierarchical levels, such as DNA sequences, genetic
circuits, and entire cells, simplifying design and analysis.

Automation: Computational tools and robotic systems aid in designing, building, and testing biological
systems more efficiently and accurately.

Applications of Synthetic Biology
Synthetic Microbes

Engineered microorganisms, such as bacteria and yeast, have been developed to produce a wide range of
high-value products, including chemicals, biofuels, pharmaceuticals, and industrial enzymes. For example:

>

Biofuel production: Genetically modified bacteria can convert plant sugars into ethanol or other
biofuels, offering a sustainable alternative to fossil fuels.

Pharmaceutical synthesis: Human insulin is now produced using Escherichia coli bacteria containing the
human insulin gene. These modified microbes synthesize insulin, which is then harvested and purified
for medical use.

Drug manufacturing: Engineered yeast strains have been used to produce artemisinin, a rey anti-
malarial compound, in a cost-effective and scalable manner.

Bioremediation: Microorganisms are engineered to degrade toxic pollutants, including oil spills and
plastic waste. Such microbes can break down environmental contaminants into less harmful
compounds, contributing to cleaner ecosystems.

Gene Editing
Gene editing technologies, such as CRISPR-Cas9, enable scientists to make precise and targeted
modifications to an organism’s DNA. Applications include:

Medical therapeutics: Correcting mutations responsible for genetic disorders such as sickle cell anemia
and cystic fibrosis.

Agricultural biotechnology: Enhancing crop yield, nutritional content, and resistance to pests, diseases,
and environmental stresses.

Livestock improvement: Producing disease-resistant animals with enhanced productivity.

Golden Rice: A prominent example of synthetic biology, Golden Rice is genetically engineered to produce
beta-carotene, a precursor of vitamin A, addressing vitamin A deficiency in developing regions.
Biomedical research: Gene editing is instrumental in generating disease models and developing novel
therapies.

Bio-Sensing Devices
Synthetic biology has facilitated the creation of highly sensitive and specific biological sensors for
environmental and biomedical applications:

Environmental monitoring: Biosensors are designed to detect pollutants such as heavy metals or toxins
in water and soil.

Medical diagnostics: Engineered cells or proteins respond to target molecules (e.g., glucose in diabetic
patients) by producing detectable signals, allowing for real-time monitoring.

Disease detection: Synthetic sensors can identify biomarkers associated with infections or cancers at
early stages, enhancing diagnostic accuracy.

Q.6 List the principles of biodefense. Why is preparedness considered a crucial principle of biodefense?

Ans. The main principles of biodefense include surveillance, detection,
prevention, preparedness, and response. Surveillance and detection
involve monitoring biological threats and identifying outbreaks early.
Prevention focuses on reducing the risk of exposure to harmful agents.
Preparedness is particularly crucial because it involves planning, training personnel, stockpiling essential
resources, and establishing protocols before an attack occurs. Effective preparedness ensures rapid
containment of pathogens, minimizes casualties, and allows efficient coordination during crises. The
response principle ensures that emergency measures are implemented to manage outbreaks effectively.
Together, these principles form a comprehensive framework to protect populations from natural,
accidental, or intentional biological threats.
