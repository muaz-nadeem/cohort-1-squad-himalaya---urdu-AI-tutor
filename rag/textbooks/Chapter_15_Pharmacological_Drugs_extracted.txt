Drugs have played a central role in human health and disease management throughout history. In biology,
the term drug refers to any chemical substance that, when taken into the body, alters its physiological processes.
Some drugs are used for therapeutic purposes to prevent, treat, or cure diseases, while others may be misused,
leading to harmful effects on health.

Pharmacological drugs are specifically those substances that act on the body’s biological systems,
particularly the nervous system, to produce characteristic effects. They may act by stimulating or depressing the
activity of certain cells, blocking or enhancing the transmission of nerve impulses, or modifying biochemical
reactions in tissues.

Understanding pharmacological drugs is essential for several reasons:

¢ Medical Importance: They form the foundation of modern medicine, enabling treatment of infections,

mental disorders, cardiovascular diseases, pain, and many other conditions.

© Scientific Relevance: Studying how drugs act helps in understanding body functions such as nerve

transmission, hormone regulation, and homeostasis.

© Social and Ethical Perspective: While therapeutic use can save lives, misuse or addiction can lead to

severe health and social problems.

Thus, pharmacological drugs occupy a unique position in human biology: they can be powerful tools for
healing when used wisely, but equally dangerous when abused or misunderstood.

DRUG DISCOVERY AND DEVELOPMENT PROCESS

A drug is a chemical substance that alters normal physiological functions of the body. In medicine, drugs are
developed to diagnose, prevent, or treat diseases. The discovery and development of new drugs is a complex,
multi-phase process that can take a decade or more, requiring collaboration across biology, chemistry,
pharmacology, and regulatory sciences. The ultimate goal is to deliver safe, effective, and high-quality
medications for human use.

1. Drug Discovery

The drug discovery stage is the foundation of the entire development process. Its purpose is to identify
biologically active compounds that can influence the course of a disease. This stage combines modern tools
of molecular biology, biochemistry, genetics, and computational sciences to search for promising drug
candidates. Since only a small fraction of potential Compounds ever become medicines, drug discovery Is

both challenging and highly selective.

The main steps include:

e Target Identification: The process begins by identifying a biological target, such as a protein, enzyme,
ion channel, receptor, or nucleic acid that plays.a crucial role in the disease mechanism. For example, in
bacterial infections, enzymes essential for cell wall synthesis may serve as drug targets. In cancer,
proteins that control cell division may be selected. A good target is one that is disease-specific and
“druggable”, meaning it can be modulated by a chemical compound.

e Target Validation: Once a potential target is identified, researchers must confirm that altering it will
actually produce a therapeutic effect. This involves laboratory experiments, genetic studies, and use of
animal models to show that blocking or activating the target improves the disease condition. For
example, validation of HIV protease as a target paved the way for protease inhibitor drugs used in
antiretroviral therapy. i

e Hit Identification: In this step, scientists look for molecules that show initial activity against the target.
This is done using high-throughput screening, a technique that tests thousands of compounds from
large chemical libraries in a short time. Molecules that show promising interactions are called “hits.”
Increasingly, computer-based methods such as virtual screening and molecular docking are also used
to predict how compounds will interact with the target, saving both time and resources.

© Hit-to-Lead Optimization: Initial hits are rarely suitable as drugs because they may lack potency, have
unwanted side effects, or be unstable in the body. Therefore, chemists modify the chemical structure of
hits to create more effective compounds known as “leads.” This process focuses on improving:
© Potency: ensuring the compound strongly affects the target at low doses.
© Specificity: reducing interaction with unintended targets to minimize side effects.
© Pharmacokinetics: optimizing how the drug is absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and excreted

(ADME properties).

o Safety and Toxicity: ensuring that the compound does not cause harmful effects in cells or tissues.
The outcome of this stage is a small number of lead compounds with the highest potential to progress into
preclinical development. These leads are then studied in greater depth for their safety, stability, and
suitability for human use.

Preclinical Development

Once lead compounds have been identified and optimized, they must be carefully studied before being
tested in humans. This stage is called preclinical development. Its purpose is to evaluate the safety,
biological activity, and drug-like properties of the compound under controlled laboratory conditions.
Preclinical studies usually take several years and provide the scientific evidence required for regulatory
authorities to permit human trials.
The main components are:
e In Vitro Studies: These are experiments performed outside a living organism, usually on cultured cells
or isolated tissues. In vitro studies help scientists understand:
© How the compound interacts with its biological target.
° Its effectiveness in blocking or stimulating a disease pathway.
o° Potential toxic effects on human cells. For example, cancer drug candidates may be tested on tumor
cell lines to measure whether they can inhibit abnormal cell growth.
© In Vivo Studies: These involve experiments in living animal models (such as mice, rats, or primates). In
vivo studies provide information about:
© Pharmacokinetics (PK): how the drug is absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and excreted in the body.
© Pharmacodynamics (PD): the biological and physiological effects of the drug, including its
mechanism of action. ¢
o Safety and efficacy: whether the compound improves disease symptoms in animals without causing
unacceptable harm.
¢ Toxicology Studies: Every potential drug must be tested for toxicity before human use. Toxicology
studies determine:
© Acute toxicity: harmful effects after a single or short-term exposure.
© Chronic toxicity: effects after repeated or long-term use.
© Safe dosage ranges: the highest dose that can be given without significant adverse effects.
© This ensures that the drug candidate has a favorable therapeutic index (the ratio between effective
dose and toxic dose).

Clinical Development

If a drug candidate successfully passes preclinical testing, the next step is to evaluate it in humans. Before

trials begin, an application such as the Investigational New Drug (IND) is submitted to regulatory authorities

(e.g., FDA in the USA, DRAP in Pakistan) for approval. Only after this clearance can clinical development—

human testing—proceed.

Clinical trials are conducted in phases, each designed to answer specific questions about the drug’s safety,

effectiveness, and appropriate use.

e Phase I: Safety and Dosage:
© Participants: 20-100 healthy volunteers (sometimes patients, if the drug is very risky, e.g., for cancer).
© Purpose: To assess safety, tolerability, dosage range, and metabolism of the drug in humans.

o. Key Question: /s the drug safe enough to be given to people, and how does the human body process it?
¢ Phase Il: Efficacy and Side Effects:
© Participants: 100-500 patients with the target disease.
O Purpose: To test the therapeutic effectiveness of the drug, refine the correct dose, and monitor
short-term side effects.
o Key Question: Does the drug work in patients, and what are its risks?
e Phase lil: Large-Scale Testing:
o Participants: 1,000-3,000 patients across multiple hospitals and countries.
© Purpose: To confirm effectiveness, compare with existing treatments, and identify less common or
long-term side effects.
o Key Question: /s the new drug better and safer than current standard treatments?
o Successful Phase III results usually form the basis for requesting regulatory approval.
Phase IV: Post-Marketing Surveillance:
o Conducted after the drug is approved and available to the public.
© Purpose: To track long-term safety and effectiveness in a much larger, diverse population. Rare
adverse reactions, interactions with other medicines, or previously unknown risks are often
discovered at this stage.
© Sometimes Phase IV studies also test new uses or formulations of the drug.

Clinical development is the most critical stage of drug testing, as it provides the final evidence of whether a

compound can become a safe and effective medicine. Although the process is long, expensive, and

sometimes results in failure, it ensures that only the best candidates are introduced for patient use.

Regulatory Review

immediately. Before reaching patients, it must pass through a stage called regulatory review, where

meet-the highest standards of safety, efficacy, and quality are approved for public use.

Post-Marketing Surveillance
Approval by regulatory authorities does not mean that a drug is completely free of risk. Once a medicine
enters the market, it is used by millions of people in diverse settings, including patients with multiple health
conditions, children, the elderly, and pregnant women—groups that may not have been fully represented in
clinical trials. For this reason, post-marketing surveillance (also called Phase IV studies) is essential to ensure
continued safety and effectiveness. *
The key elements include:
© Monitoring:
o Long-term safety tracking is carried out through reporting systems where doctors, pharmacists, and
patients can submit information about side effects.
o. This helps identify rare, delayed, or unexpected adverse reactions that may not have appeared in
earlier trials because of limited sample sizes or shorter duration.
© For example, some painkillers and anti-diabetic drugs have been withdrawn from the market after
serious cardiovascular side effects were discovered post-approval.
¢ Additional Studies:
© Post-marketing research may investigate:
= New therapeutic uses of the drug (e.g., aspirin, originally developed as a painkiller, later found
to reduce the risk of blood clots).
= New dosage forms such as slow-release tablets, syrups, or injections.
= Combination therapies, where the drug is tested with other medicines to improve effectiveness
or reduce side effects.
© These studies can expand the usefulness of a drug while ensuring patient safety.
Post-marketing surveillance is the final layer of protection in the drug development process. It ensures that
medicines remain safe and effective under real-world conditions, and it provides valuable data that can
guide improvements, new indications, or even withdrawal of unsafe products.

CLASSES OF ANTIBIOTICS AND THEIR MODE OF ACTION

Antibiotics are chemical substances, either naturally produced by microorganisms or synthetically
manufactured, that inhibit the growth of.or kill other microorganisms, especially bacteria. They are grouped into
different classes based on their chemical structure and mechanism of action.

Here are four important classes:

1. Penicillins
¢ Definition: Penicillins were the first true antibiotics discovered, marking a revolution in medicine. They
were originally derived from the mold Penicillium notatum by Alexander Fleming in 1928 and later mass-
produced during World War Il. They belong to the B-lactam class of antibiotics (so named because they
contain a B-lactam ring —a special four-sided chemical structure essential for their action).

*@ Mode of Action: Penicillins specifically target the bacterial cell wall (the tough outer layer that protects

bacterial cells, not present in human cells, which makes the drug safe for us).

© They block the enzyme transpeptidase (also called penicillin-binding protein, PBP — a bacterial
enzyme that helps “stitch” the wall together).

© Normally, this enzyme cross-links peptidoglycan chains (peptidoglycan = a mesh-like sugar + protein
material that gives bacteria their rigid shape).

© Without cross-linking, the cell wall becomes weak and leaky.

©. Under pressure, the bacteria burst and die.

©. This killing effect makes penicillins bactericidal (they kill bacteria outright, instead of just slowing
them down).

© They are especially effective against Gram-positive bacteria (bacteria with a thick cell wall, e.g.,
Streptococcus).

e. Examples and Uses:

© Penicillin G: The original penicillin; effective against pneumonia, strep throat, and syphilis.

o Amoxicillin: A semi-synthetic version (chemically improved form), stable and effective for ear,
throat, and urinary tract infections.

oO Ampicillin: Broader spectrum than Penicillin G, can act against some Gram-negative bacteria (Gram-
negative = bacteria with thinner walls but an extra outer membrane, e.g., E. coli).

© Clinical importance:

© Before penicillin, simple infections like pneumonia or infected wounds often caused death.

© The discovery of penicillin turned once-deadly diseases into treatable conditions and laid the
foundation for modern antibiotics.

2. Tetracyclines
© Definition: Tetracyclines are a class of broad-spectrum antibiotics (meaning they can act against a wide

range of bacteria, both Gram-positive and Gram-negative).

o They were first discovered in the 1940s and remain widely used because of their effectiveness.

© They are particularly useful against respiratory infections, acne, urinary tract infections, and some
diseases caused by unusual bacteria (e.g., Chlamydia, Rickettsia, and Mycoplasma).

© Mode of Action: Unlike penicillins (which attack the bacterial wall), tetracyclines target bacterial protein

production, which is essential for survival.

© They bind to the 30S ribosomal subunit (a smaller part of the bacterial ribosome — the ribosome is

like a "protein-making factory" inside the cell).

© This blocks the attachment of aminoacyl-tRNA (the “carrier molecules” that bring amino acids, the
building blocks of proteins, to the ribosome).
Without this step, the elongation of the protein chain (the process of making longer proteins) is stopped.
© Since bacteria cannot make proteins, they stop growing and multiplying.
© This makes tetracyclines bacteriostatic (they stop bacterial growth rather than killing bacteria directly).

ANTIVIRALS AND ANTIRETROVIRALS

Antiviral drugs are designed to treat infections caused by viruses, Unlike bacteria, viruses rely entirely on the
host's cellular machinery for replication, making them more difficult to target.

Antivirals

Antiviral drugs inhibit viral replication by interfering with various stages of the viral life cycle, such as
attachment, entry, genome replication, or protein synthesis. These drugs are used against a broad range of viruses
including influenza viruses, herpesviruses, and hepatitis viruses.

_

Monoclonal antibodies are laboratory-produced molecules that mimic the immune system's ability to
recognize and neutralize specific pathogens or abnormal cells. These antibodies are derived from a single clone of
cells and bind to a single specific antigen.
Mechanism of Action

Monoclonal antibodies are engineered to recognize and bind very precisely to a specific antigen (a unique
marker, usually a protein) found on the surface of pathogens (like viruses and bacteria) or abnormal human cells
(like cancer cells). Once bound, they can act in multiple ways:
1, Direct Neutralization

e The antibody binds to a harmful molecule or pathogen and blocks its activity.

e Example: mAbs used in viral infections can stop viruses from attaching to host cells.
2. Immune System Activation

© By attaching to the target cell, mAbs act like a “flag” or signal to the immune system.

© This attracts immune cells (like macrophages and natural killer cells) to destroy the marked cell.
3. Complement Activation —__

© Binding of mAbs can trigger the complement system (a group of blood proteins that punch holes in cell

membranes).

¢ This leads to the destruction of the targeted cell.
4. Blocking Growth Signals

¢ Some cancer cells grow uncontrollably because of signals from receptors on their surface.

® mAbs can block these receptors, preventing cancer cells from receiving “grow and divide” messages.
5. Delivery of Toxic Agents

*® mAbs can be linked to toxins, chemotherapy drugs, or radioactive substances.

© Once the antibody finds its target, it delivers the toxic “payload” directly to diseased cells, sparing most

: healthy ones.

The mechanism of monoclonal antibodies is not limited to one function—they can neutralize, tag, block, or
directly kill diseased cells. Their high precision makes them powerful tools against cancer, autoimmune diseases,
and infections.

Monoclonal Antibodies vs Other Therapeutic Agents
Compared to Antibiotics

Antibiotics act broadly on bacterial processes (e.g., cell wall synthesis, protein synthesis) and can affect
a wide range of bacterial species.

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), however, are precisely targeted against one specific antigen, which
reduces off-target effects (unwanted harm to beneficial bacteria or healthy cells).

mAbs are also less prone to resistance development, because they target unique proteins rather than
general bacterial machinery.

Layman’s view: Antibiotics are like “pesticides” that kill many bugs (good and bad), while mAbs are like
“snipers” that only hit the exact harmful target.

Compared to Polyclonal Antibodies

Polyclonal antibodies are natural mixtures produced by the immune system, binding to multiple
epitopes (different parts of the same antigen).

This leads to variability between batches and less precise targeting.

Monoclonal antibodies bind to only one epitope (a single, unique spot on the antigen), ensuring
superior specificity and consistency.

Layman’s view: Polyclonal antibodies are like a group of archers shooting arrows in different directions,
while monoclonal antibodies are one expert archer hitting the bull’s-eye every time.

Compared to Chemotherapy and Radiotherapy

.

Chemotherapy and radiotherapy kill rapidly dividing cells, but they cannot distinguish cancer cells from
healthy dividing cells, leading to side effects such as hair loss, nausea, and immune suppression.
Monoclonal antibody therapies can selectively bind only to cancer-specific antigens, which allows:

© Lower required dosages.

o Reduced collateral damage to healthy tissues.

Layman’s view: Traditional therapies are like “carpet bombing” (hitting everything in the area), while
mAbs are like “guided missiles” targeting only enemy bases.

Compared to Small-Molecule Drugs

Many small-molecule drugs are rapidly cleared from the body and have unpredictable
pharmacokinetics (how the body absorbs, distributes, metabolizes, and eliminates them).

Monoclonal antibodies, especially IgG type, have long half-lives (~21 days), meaning they stay active
in the body much longer and require less frequent dosing.

Their action is also more predictable.

Q.7 How do antibiotics work?

Ans. Antibiotics are chemical agents that target bacteria to either kill them

Q.8 Why are monoclonal antibodies so specific?

specificity arises because all antibody molecules in a monoclonal
preparation are identical clones derived from a single parent B-cell. The
precise interaction ensures that mAbs act only on the intended target, such as a cancer cell receptor ora
pathogenic protein, while leaving other cells and tissues unaffected. This high specificity reduces off-target
effects and side effects compared to traditional drugs, making monoclonal antibodies particularly valuable
in targeted therapy, diagnostics, and immunotherapy for diseases such as cancer, autoimmune disorders,
and viral infections.
